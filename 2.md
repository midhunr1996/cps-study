## Fundamentals & Definition

### What is a requirement?
- **Requirement = an accurately phrased development target for a property** of the system or the component, **considering all relevant conditions**.
- **Amount:** depends on product complexity, but in general **several hundred to (ten-)thousands** of requirements.
- Abbreviation: **Requirement Management = RM**.
- The amount of requirements keeps **increasing over time** (e.g. cars — autonomous driving adds many new requirements on top of the standard ones).

### Requirements are defined in standards
- **For software:** **ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148** — *Systems and software engineering — Life cycle processes — Requirements engineering*.
- **ISO/IEC 25000** — *Systems and software Quality Requirements and Evaluation (SQuaRE)*.
- Part of the **maturity models**: **CMMI**, **SPICE (ISO/IEC 15504)**, **ISO/IEC 12207**.

### Anchoring in system design — ItO (Idea to Offer)
- **ItO – Idea to Offer (simplified)** is the **core process of development** — from the requirement, through various ideas and concepts, to implementation.
- At the same time, **purchasing** ensures procurement of materials, tools, systems…
- After successful **testing & validation (verification)** and **industrialization**, the product can be **released for production**.
- Flow: **customer requirement → ItO → offer → product**.
- **Aim:** meet all requirements best and develop the **optimal product**.

## Why Requirement Management?

The famous "tree-swing" cartoon shows how every department misunderstands the requirement (how the customer explained it · how the project leader understood it · how the designer drew it · what was assembled · what the customer *really* needed…). RM exists to close that gap.

- RM is **responsible to identify the necessary requirements** from **(super)system level down to component level**.
- RM is about **maintaining all requirements over the whole lifecycle**.
- **RM is the enabler to:**
  - increase **customer satisfaction**
  - get a **common understanding** between customer and supplier
  - **avoid safety issues / field issues** (quality)
  - avoid **late changes and cost impacts**
  - feed **LeLe (lessons learned)**
- ➡ RM is the **essential discipline in systems design**: *if the targets are set wrong, the whole product is wrong!*

### Challenges for RM
- **Clear and distinct** definition of requirements.
- **Completeness** — missing requirements that are only uncovered later.
- **Inaccurate interpretation** of requirements (the description may not be exact enough).
- ➡ **LeLe is important!**

## Core Documents

There are **two main documents** that must be delivered at the project milestones (shown in the process):
1. **Product Requirements Document (PRD)**
2. **Implementation Specification**

### Product Requirements Document (PRD) — German: *Lastenheft*
- Described in **DIN 69901-5**, or by the **IEEE as SRS (Software Requirements Specification, 29148)**.
- **Sum of all requirements of a commercial customer** (not mandatorily the end customer / user of the product).
- Used in the **nomination phase** of a product by customers to **request a technical and commercial offer** from their suppliers.

**PRD content:**
- Introduction
- Description of the **current state** (Where are we?)
- Description of the **target** → main requirements
- Description of **interfaces**
- **Functional requirements**
- **Nonfunctional requirements (NFR)**
- **System architecture**
- **Expected scope of delivery**
- **Acceptance criteria**

**PRD detail level:**
- **Internal development** → PRD is **more detailed and tiered down**.
- **External development** → keep it on a **more generic property level** to **avoid specifying the solution**.
- The supplier **answers with an Implementation Specification**.
- Normally takes **several rounds of clarification & updating** of the PRD with all suppliers; the customer then **chooses the best solution** from the different offers.

### Implementation Specification — German: *Pflichtenheft*
*(also: System specification / Scope statement / …)*
- The **answer of a supplier to the customer's PRD**.
- Contains the:
  - **"How"** to realize the customer requirements → **technical concept**
  - **"With what"** to realize them → **resources, project plan, …**
- **Every requirement in the PRD** must be **covered by one or more deliverables** in the system specification.
- With the **customer's acceptance** of the implementation specification, **development starts**.

## The RM Process

Different process models for requirement management exist; variants are described in:
- **IEEE (SWEBOK)**
- **CMMI** (Capability Maturity Model Integration)
- **IIBA (BABOK)** — International Institute of Business Analysis
- **Volere**
- **IREB** — International Requirements Engineering Board

The lecture shows **one variant**. Two key milestones in it:
- **PRD freeze**
- **Implementation specification freeze**

In larger system developments, a **requirement manager** steers the whole process.

| Level | Request count | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Customer | request | Customer |

## (1) Collecting Requests

**Target:** gather **all relevant requests** from internal or external sources.

### External requests, e.g.
- **!!!Customer!!!** → this **should and must be the prior source!**
- **Law**
- **Standards & guidelines**
- **Cultural expectations**
- **Political expectations**
- **Social expectations**

> **Interview note:** the gained knowledge **decreases as the number of interview partners increases** (diminishing returns).

### Use cases (formalising customer demand)
- Customer demand can be formalised in **use cases**; complex use cases may use **diagrams**.
- Use cases are **named after the respective goal**.
- Format (see also product backlog):
  - **As…** [role / who]
  - **I would like to…** [wants what / function]
  - **To…** [reason / target]

### Internal requests
- **LeLe (Lessons Learned)**
- **Standards** — e.g. company standards
- **Existing tool kits** — e.g. we must use motor A or B, nothing else
- **Company strategy** — e.g. no sourcing outside our country
- **CI (corporate identity) / design** — e.g. all products need green rounded corners
- (Business-department restrictions)

### LeLe — Lessons Learned (collecting requests)
- At the **start of the project**, knowledge from previous projects must be used (**knowledge management / lessons learned – LeLe**).
- During a **kick-off workshop**, project members get familiar with previously gathered knowledge to **avoid known errors** and **implement best practices**.
- Knowledge management covers **all areas** within a project:
  - **Technical knowledge** (how to design the component, wire routing, …)
  - **Project-management knowledge** (duration of processes, …)
  - **Commercial topics**
- Should be **documented in software**.
- LeLe documentation must be **constantly updated by all project members** during the project.
- At the **end of the project**, hold a workshop with all members to transfer findings (**what went well, what needs improvement**) into the **new standard** for follow-up projects.

> **⚠ Warning:** the solution to a problem is **not just the negation of the issue!**
> E.g. *"We started the test too late"* → *"We must not start the test so late"* does **not** help the follow-up project. **Better:** *"Start the test at least 2 weeks before the release and anchor it in the project plan."* The same applies to positive feedback.

**LeLe documentation columns:**

| ID | From | Date | Keep / Improve | Topic | Priority | Suggested Solution / Sustainable Anchored | Responsible | Implementation until | Status | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 001 | Jens Koppi, LM-2 | 10.12.2022 | keep | parallel ordering while simulation is running saved us 2 weeks development time | high | Change generic project plan | Franz Haupt, AM-1 | 13.08.2025 | realized | |
| 002 | Patricia Müller, TE-8 | 10.12.2022 | improve | Prototype wrongly built, causing a test delay | middle | provide assembly manual early enough for the prototype workshop | Jan Schulze, OF-2 | 20.10.2025 | clarification needed | |

Key column meanings: **ID** = unique ID for the topic · **From** = who brought the input (for follow-up questions) · **Topic** = detailed description of what to keep/improve (NOT the project topic) · **Suggested Solution** = countermeasure(s) to improve or sustainably anchor the "keep" (all future projects do the same) · **Priority** = priority of handling · **Responsible** = who implements the change/anchoring · **Status** = overall status.
*(Template: "xxx_Template_LeLe_YYMMDD.xlsx".)*

> **Exercise:** Run a Lessons Learned for a self-chosen project (e.g. repainting a room, a soccer competition, a round-the-world trip). 15 min fill the table (several topics), 5 min discuss with a neighbour whether each is a feasible long-term solution.

### Standards (a type of collected request)
- **Regional:** TGL (German DDR industry standard), **DIN** (German BRD), **GB / GB/T** (China).
- **International:** **EN** — European standard · **ISO** — International (mechanics) · **IEC** — International electrical & electronics.
- **Industry:** **IATF** — International Automotive Task Force (automotive) · **VDE** — Verband der Elektrotechnik Elektronik Informationstechnik e.V.

### "Who is the customer?" pitfall
- A wrong requirement can come from **misunderstanding who the customer is** (the cartoon of parents choosing for the child — "I think he likes it" — while ignoring the actual user). Always set requirements for the **real customer/user**.

## (2) Assess Requests

Assess requests by **Relevance**, **Priority**, and **Classification**.

### Relevance
- Is this request **relevant for this specific product?**
- E.g. *Will this product be sold in Japan? If not, we don't need to follow Japanese law or Japanese customer expectations.*

### Priority
- If **not all requests can be fulfilled**, which one has the **higher priority** (e.g. safety)?
  - (A) the customer can use the turn signal in every situation
  - (B) in a front crash, the driver airbag must open
  - → Would you allow the turn signal to fail because the crash system permanently has higher priority — even when there is currently no crash? (Shows priority conflicts are subtle.)
- Priority also depends on **company strategy & focus**. The resulting product can look completely different because requirements **negatively impact each other** (e.g. "easy to maintain" needs more space and costs).

**Possible (design) strategies:**
- **Design for Customer** — e.g. by Design Thinking (see *Ideation* chapter)
- **Design for Costs** — e.g. business-case centred
- **Design for Quality and Reliability**
- **Design for Production / Manufacturing (DFM)** — e.g. easy to assemble
- **Design for Service** — e.g. easy to maintain

### Kano Model
- A method to **prioritise customer requests**, by **Prof. Dr. Noriaki Kano**.
- Describes the **connection between an implemented feature and customer satisfaction**.
- Evolved out of **quality management**.
- The model has axes **customer satisfaction** (dissatisfied ↔ satisfied) vs **realised quality** (low ↔ high).

**The 5 levels of requests (Kano):**

| Level | Customer view | Behaviour | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basic needs (must)** | Natural & self-evident; only noticed when missing | Not fulfilling → dissatisfaction; fulfilling → **no** extra satisfaction. Differentiation from competitors nearly impossible. | Greeting of a customer in a call centre |
| **Performance needs (should)** | Recognised by the customer | Satisfaction **scales with degree of fulfilment** (more is better / removes dissatisfaction) | Waiting time in the hotline |
| **Delighter (could)** | Not expected by the customer | Creates **enthusiasm**; small increase → **disproportionally** large positive benefit | Online recording of the reported incident with live status tracking |
| **Indifferent** | — | Existing or not has **no / low impact** on satisfaction | Chosen song of the waiting jingle in the hotline |
| **Reverse (don't)** | — | If present → **lowers** satisfaction; if missing → does not cause satisfaction | Using technical phrases in a hotline call |

**Classification of a request is not fixed — it differs by:**
- **Customer** — a delighter for customer 1 can be a reverse for customer 2.
- **Pricing / business model** — a delighter for a low-budget product can be a basic need for a premium product.
- **Time** — delighters become basic needs as the state of the art evolves.

**Getting customer feedback (Kano survey):**
- Gather feedback via **structured interview** or **written survey**.
- The survey must be **standardised** to remove the impact of the interviewer.
- Should include **demographic data** to analyse correlations (e.g. girls 3–12 rate "pink" as a performance need, while men 50–70 rate it as reverse). This enables **variants / tailoring** and **identification of peer groups**.

**Kano survey — the functional/dysfunctional question pair:**

| | | I like it | I expect it | I am neutral | I can tolerate it | I dislike it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | How would you feel if the product had / has **more** of …? | | | | | |
| **Dysfunctional** | How would you feel if the product did **not** have / **less** of …? | | | | | |

**Deriving the class from the answer combination:**

| Functional answer | Dysfunctional answer | Class |
|---|---|---|
| I expect it | I dislike it | **basic needs (must)** |
| I like it | I dislike it | **performance needs (should)** |
| I like it | I am neutral | **delighter (could)** |
| I am neutral | I am neutral | **indifferent** |
| I dislike it | I expect it | **reverse (don't)** |
| All others (e.g. both answered "I like it") | | **Remove or questionable** |

> **Kano example (car):** indifferent = colour of the underfloor screw · performance (should) = driving performance / horsepower · reverse (don't) = new rust spots after a car wash · delighter (could) = launch-control mode · basic need (must) = the car can drive.

### Classification of requests
**By "Belonging":**
- **Product requirements (What)** — *[focus for System Engineering]* — product/system-related, e.g. performance, size, reliability, transportability, user-friendliness…
- **Process requirements (How)** — e.g. implementation, methods…
- **External requirements (Rest)** — everything not assignable to a product or process (e.g. the development process).

**By "Function / NFR":**
- **Functional requirements** — e.g. the system should print my file.
- **Non-functional requirements (NFR)** — not dedicated features (don't create a purpose of the system) but still expectations, e.g. the system is available 24/7 with an uptime of 99.9%.

**Attributes of a function:**
- What are the **inputs and outputs**?
- **Range of values / dimensions** for input/output?
- **Accepted margin / tolerance** for input/output?
- **How often** is this function executed?
- **How fast** must results be available (**throughput time**)?

## (3) Develop Requirements

### Requirements need to be…
- **Complete** — no assumptions by the supplier about the intended system.
- **Correct** — without errors of content.
- Set **SMART** (see below), **testable**, **linked**.
- **Necessary** by minimum one party — the system shouldn't have any "nice to haves".
- **Short sentences**.
- **Atomic** — only **one requirement per sentence**.
- Written as **active** sentences (*"The motor rotates the shaft."*) not passive (*"The shaft is driven by the motor."*).
- **Without weak words** like "really, very, roughly…"; use tools to identify them, e.g. **ReQualize** or **DESIRe**.
- **Avoid** words like "should, must…".
- **Understandable** for supplier and developer.
- **Traceable** — every implemented function/NFR linked to the requirement that caused it.
- **Unambiguous & consistent** — requirements don't contradict each other; otherwise prioritise between them.
- **Feasible** based on the resources given.
- **Equipped with properties** (see below).

### Properties of a requirement
- **Unique identifier** for traceability (e.g. number)
- **Requirement description**
- **Rationale / Target** — reason for the requirement
- **Originator** — who is requesting this?
- **Creator** — who wrote the requirement?
- **User** — who uses the resulting feature?
- **Fit Criterion** — testable criteria
- **Priority** — for decisions under constraints (time, budget) and conflicting requirements
- **Dependencies & interfaces** of requirements, functions, processes, objects…
- **Conflicting requirements**
- **Definition of release / maturity** (clarity of availability)
- **Linked documents** — law, standards, explaining documents…
- **Comments**
- **Version**
- **Change History**
- **Status** (accepted, under review, update needed…)

### Linked
Requirements need to be **linked** to their…
- **Requests** — what was the initial background?
- **Child & mother requirements** — to see hierarchy dependencies (system requirement → underlying component requirements)
- **"Neighbour requirements"** — requirements that impact each other or can only be fulfilled together
- **Functions & NFR** — to see what caused this function
- **Test items** — to see if the requirement was successfully tested
- **Interfaces** — where to get/send data, mechanical interfaces…

> **Linking failure example (real):** a bridge over a river was built from **Germany** (referenced to the **North Sea** as zero) and **Switzerland** (referenced to the **Mediterranean Sea** as zero), causing a **28 cm height difference**. The correction was even done in the **wrong direction** (so doubled) instead of being compensated. ➡ Requirements that aren't linked cause expensive errors.

### SMART
Goals/requirements should be written in a **"SMART"** way:
- **S**pecific
- **M**easurable
- **A**chievable
- **R**easonable
- **T**ime-Bound

**Why specific matters — natural language is ambiguous.**
- *"I saw the man on the mountain with the telescope."* can be parsed **many** ways (who has the telescope, who is on the mountain?). Different bracketings = different pictures.
- The "I need a ruler" / "How long?" / "5 minutes!" gag shows the same ambiguity.
- **Possible solutions:** express requirements in a **"mathematical" description** like structured code (**IF–THEN–ELSE**), and **state which variants are excluded**.

**Why not too specific?**
- Don't over-constrain — leave room for the **best solution**.
- **Good-specification example:** a company asked to **reduce shipping damage** of a bicycle (NOT to "improve the package"). The team designed a shipping box **looking like a TV**, which significantly reduced damage. *(Specifying the* problem *— not the solution — let a clever solution emerge.)*

### Testable
- A requirement must be **testable**. To be testable it must be:
  - **Specific**
  - **Detailed**
  - **Affordable to test**
  - **Linked to a maturity level** (see phase 5)
- Needs to be **specific enough** (see SMART). E.g. *"The charger needs to be efficient"* is not testable — is 75% or 80% enough?
- Verification must be **affordable**. E.g. *"The radio mute button must be accessible after a crash"* — you don't want to crash several (statistically significant) cars just to verify this.

### General guideline for writing requirements

| Guideline | Bad | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Positive instead of negative | The box is not complicated to open. | The box is easy to open. |
| Avoid "could, should…" | If possible, the bike should be easy to clean. | The bike is easy to clean. |
| Specific | The cage is robust. | The cage operates normally after 10× dropping from 2 m height onto concrete. |
| What, not how | The bag has a handle on the top. | The bag has a possibility to carry it. |

### SCRUM / product backlog (agile variant)
- **SCRUM** is an **agile project-management** approach mainly used in **software development**.
- Follows a **similar idea** of describing requirements but **highly simplified**: from an **Epic → feature request** stored in the **product backlog**.

**Example product backlog:**

| ID | As… [Role] | I would like to [Function] | to [Reason] | Priority | Story Points [Effort] | Sprint # | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Administrator | delete comments | delete hate speech | necessity | 1 | 1 | Closed |
| 4 | User | change my mail preferences | avoid getting flooded by spam | necessity | 5 | 1 | Closed |
| 5 | Visitor | create an account | read articles | should | 2 | 2 | In Progress |
| 6 | User | contact the administrator | solve problems with my account | should | 3 | 2 | In Progress |
| 7 | Administrator | create categories | give users the chance to create new content | could | 5 | | Open |

## The System Wheel

> *Recap from chapter 1 — used here to develop core requirements as a "datasheet".*

The **System Wheel** gives an overview of **typical properties of a mechatronic system** that should be taken care of. It is layered:
- **Realization domain (centre):** mechanics · E/E · software
- **Core system properties "DNA" (data sheet):** performance · consumption · acoustic · costs · reliability · weight · size
- **Extended system properties (10 "Wirkketten"):** acoustical behaviour · performance & efficiency · data acquisition, access & security · design for production, service & upgrade capability · safety · EMC · sustainability · reliability · thermal behaviour · volumetric behaviour & design

### Role of the property engineer
- The **property engineer translates the customer requirement into system properties** for **his specific property** (not all) → he/she is the **voice of the customer** for that property.
- Target: **overarching property optimisation** based on the technical solution kits provided by the component teams in the realization domain — **not a single-component optimum**.
- Think in **system functions** (what should the system be able to do) — **not** in components or, worse, the company's organizational structure.
- The property engineer **breaks the function down to component requirements**, while realization domains offer **different technical solutions**.
- Properties in systems are **connected and impact each other**; the **system engineer decides in case of conflicting targets**.

### System Wheel deep-dive (typical properties per extended field)
- **Acoustical behaviour:** emission noise (air & structure-borne); emission of vibrations; active noise cancellation; "positive" sound design.
- **Performance & efficiency:** power loss & efficiency; control behaviour & interference immunity; repeatability (load jumps, cycles); provision of mechanical (speed, torque) & electrical (current, voltage) power; operating ranges; accuracy; dynamics; target/actual comparison; avoidance of overshoot.
- **Data acquisition, access & security:** security strategy; ECU security; cybersecurity; mechanical security; secure communication; flashing & coding strategy; data integrity.
- **Design for production, service & upgrade capability:** ease to assemble (few, simple, automatable steps); mechanical stability; material supply at the line; assembly direction/handling; reproducible/safe process; technical cleanness (TecSa); easy to repair/service/maintain; diagnostic concept (OTA vs physical, self-diagnosis & healing, legally required diagnoses); subsequent HW/SW updates → lifecycle management; self-configuration & training; self-optimisation (AI).
- **Safety:** functional safety concept (FuSiKo), GuR/HARA; technical security concept (TeSiKo); safety management; **ISO 26262**-compliant documentation; electrical safety (overvoltage prevention, passive & active discharge, HV interlock, safe state on low supply voltage, robustness against voltage fluctuations); mechanical safety; thermal safety.
- **EMC (electromagnetic compatibility):** interference emission; noise immunity; ESD (electrostatic discharge).
- **Sustainability** (whole product lifecycle): end of life ("grave"); easily recyclable materials; reuse of components.
- **Reliability:** startup behaviour; avoidance of shock/vibration emission; avoidance of electrical interference (voltage ripple); emergency operation & error handling (what is kept alive/shut down and in which priority, substitute functions, triggering by/for other components); lifetime; corrosion; chemical resistance (oil, cleaner…); insulation stability; protection against external impacts; derating; overvoltage protection circuits.
- **Thermal behaviour:** active heating/cooling; fire protection; thermal operational safety; thermal degradation; heat creation & emission to the environment (impact on neighbouring components or humans).
- **Volumetric behaviour & design:** look & feel / UX, stylish housing; geometric integration (fitting into a given space); size; weight; tolerance concept of interconnecting components (**FMK – Funktionsmaßkonzept**); crash behaviour (mechanical deformation, software/electrical shutdown…).

> **Exercise:** develop a full requirement set (datasheet format) for a **tea candle** based on the system wheel — each group gets the same amount of properties, and requirements include all the properties of a requirement.

## (4) Tier Down

- **Tier down** of system requirements to **lower levels** (e.g. component level).
- You must end up with a description that the **component team in the realization domain can develop and take responsibility for**.
- **Don't underestimate this:** e.g. the developer of a loudspeaker system **won't take accountability for the sound inside a car** — he doesn't develop the room/surroundings.
- The **receiver** (the role responsible to fulfil the requirement, e.g. the component-development team) must **accept, reject, or feedback a request for change**.

### Process of a new requirement (decision flow)
1. **Start** — a new requirement is **provided into the inbox / pool** by a higher system level.
2. **Requirement acceptable?**
   - **Yes** → **add requirement** to the component requirements → **End**.
   - **No** → **Modification possible?**
     - **Yes** → **modify requirement** (loop back) and/or **requirement declined with reason**.
     - **No** → **discard requirement** → **End**.

### System hierarchy (tier-down levels)
**Supersystem (4) → System (3) → Sub-system (2) → Component (1).** Requirements cascade down this hierarchy.

### (5) Track validation / verification of requirements

- RM is **not in charge of the verification itself**, but of **tracking** whether the requirement was **successfully confirmed by testing**.

- **Changes of requests & requirements** must be **tracked and followed up**.

## Software Providers

### Software functions needed
Professional software is needed (**no standard office tool!**) to:
- **Administrate** requirements (create, change…)
- **Structure, sort and filter** them
- **Tailor** requirements to the specific owner (e.g. department)
- Manage **workflow & status** (accept, discard…)
- **Link** requirements to see dependencies
- Provide **change history & versioning**
- Support **branching** for different products
- **Traceability and transparency** for project members is very important.

### ReqIF (Requirement Interchange Format)
- **ReqIF = Requirement Interchange Format**.
- An **XML data format**.
- Managed by the **ProSTEP iViP e.V.**
- Designed to **exchange requirements between different software** — and between **customer & supplier**.

### Example tools
- **Codebeamer (PTC)** — https://www.ptc.com/de/products/codebeamer
- **DOORS (IBM)** — https://www.ibm.com/de-de/products/requirements-management
- **JAMA software** — https://www.jamasoftware.com/
- Long lists of tools: systemsengineeringtools.com · makingofsoftware.com · volere.org.

### Use in software development (agile)
- In **agile software development** (e.g. **SCRUM**), **no requirement-specific tools** are needed — the project setup itself forces **requirement-driven development**.
- In SCRUM: **Epic ("System Level") → product backlog ("component level") → sprint backlog ("development")**.
- Example tools: **ClickUp** · **cplace** · **Jira**.

---

## Summary

- **Requirement management is the most important step within system design.**
- RM is necessary for the **right product, with the right quality**, to ensure the **highest customer satisfaction**.
- RM can be divided into **6 phases** and should **focus on properties, not on technical solutions**:
  1. Collect requests → 2. Assess requests → 3. Develop requirements → 4. Tier down → 5. Validation & Verification → 6. Follow up of changes.
- *If the targets are set wrong, the whole product is wrong* — but don't slip into analysis paralysis; start realizing.
