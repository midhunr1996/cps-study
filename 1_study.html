📖 The Full Story — “The Build”

The whole chapter as one connected tale. Don't try to memorize it — just read it like a story and let it run as a film in your head. Because each thing happens for a reason, the concepts stick by themselves. Exam words are in bold; the table at the end maps every beat to the point you write in the exam.

It's a grey Monday in the workshop and your team has exactly one idea, written on a coffee-stained napkin: build a robot that cleans the floor. You grab a screwdriver — and a wise owl named Cy glides down, lands right on the napkin, and stops your hand with a wing.

“Before you touch a single screw,” she hoots, “split that word in two.” You're doing System Design, and that is always two halves: the System — the purpose, the WHAT Robi is for — and the Design — the HOW, the wiring and code that make the purpose real. Robi's purpose is clean the floor; everything else is just the how. You write WHAT, then HOW on the napkin and feel slightly less lost.

“Fine,” you say, “but what even is a system?” Cy sweeps her wing around the room. “A system is a group of different parts working together for one shared purpose, with a fence you can draw around it to separate it from the world.” The magic word is different: if your parts were all identical with no shared goal, you'd have a pile — an accumulation — not a system. She points out the window at a galaxy, a leaf, a lung — grown with no human hand, natural systems — then at the bench: a piano, an airplane, a robot — built by people, artificial systems. Robi is about to join that second list.

Cy isn't done sorting. She drops a coral-reef tank on the bench — living coral plus machine pumps, a living-and-built mix — to show systems come in three flavours: natural · artificial · biotechnical, and the man-made ones split again into technical (machines), social (a family, a team) and sociotechnical (the two tangled together). Then she taps three little doors on Robi's shell: one wide open (full exchange of info/material/energy), one a mail-slot passing only a trickle of pre-defined data (like a car's OBD port = partly open), and one welded shut (isolated). Even inside Robi the doors differ: his tracking system is isolated, but the map he draws leaves through an open Bluetooth door.

Now, why is it called a Cyber-Physical System? Cy explains that “cyber” is short for cybernetics — the control of machines using feedback, copied from how living things use their senses and how people use conversation to course-correct. She sketches Robi's brain as a control loop: a target value (“where I should be”) minus the current value (“where I am”) makes a gap; the controller fires a command to an actuator; the world reacts; a sensor measures the result and feeds it back, and round it spins. “That feedback brain is the cyber half,” she says; “the body is the physical half.” She warns you not to confuse Robi with a lonely mechatronic machine like a washing machine (software + hardware, sealed, standalone). The moment that washer starts texting the solar panels to run when the sun shines, it joins a networked smart home — and that's a CPS (distributed, internet-connected, IoT). There's no sharp line between the two; a CPS is just mechatronics that talks.

To keep all of Robi's qualities straight, Cy spins up the course's master diagram: the System Wheel, a great bicycle wheel. Its hub is Robi's “DNA” — a one-page datasheet (performance, consumption, cost, weight, size). The spokes are extra properties (acoustics, safety, EMC, thermal, sustainability…). The rim is painted in three colours for the three trades that actually build him — mechanics, E/E and software (about a third each in a modern product). And standing at the very edge, turning the wheel, is the customer, who raises requirements at system level.

Robi has to do something, so Cy reveals every system's secret shape: a conveyor belt. Formally it's SIPOC (Supplier · Input · Process · Output · Customer), and as hardware it reads Sensor → Connector → Logic → Connector → Actuator/Visualizer. A sensor feels the floor, a connector (wire/PCB/wireless) carries the signal, the logic (software/IC/AI) decides, another connector carries the command, and an actuator (motor/valve/screen) acts. Even ChatGPT fits: prompt box (sensor) → server (connector) → “AI magic” (logic) → browser (connector) → answer field (visualizer).

Robi is getting big, so you pull him apart like Russian nesting dolls: a rare outer Supersystem holds Systems, which hold Sub-systems, which hold Components, and every higher level is just the sum of the dolls inside. Two dolls always exist — the System and the Component — while Supersystem and Sub-system are optional, and each lower level must hold more than one part or it wouldn't be a separate level at all. The spooky bit: the same doll is a different level depending on who's holding it. To a car-maker the car is the system and a screw is a tiny component; to the screw supplier the screw IS the system. Industry calls these supplier layers tiers (manufacturer → tier-1 → tier-2 → …).

Tip the smallest dolls onto the bench and you get Robi's components — the basic elements. Some are real and touchable: hardware (electronics, mechanics, hydraulics), software (functions, interfaces) and material/consumables (chemicals, liquids). Some are immaterial: the talents & roles of your team and their ideas. Every component is fenced off and does its own job.

Then something eerie happens. You drop one crumb of sulphur — colourless. You pour a pile — and it glows yellow. The colour belongs to the crowd, not the crumb. Cy calls it emergence: a system has properties that aren't in any single part — “1 + 1 = 3.” (One molecule has no pressure; a roomful of them, a gas, does.) But she wags a feather in warning: if something only looks magic because you don't understand it yet, that's just ignorance, not emergence — it vanishes the day science catches up.

A storm cloud rolls over the workshop, and Cy names the modern world VUCA: Volatile (wild swings), Uncertain (unclear, unpredictable outcomes), Complex (tangled in many layers), Ambiguous (many readings, no clean description). She plays a quick game — a subway map versus the weather: which is complex? Trick question. The map is only complicated — hard, but every rule is known, so you can predict it and a waterfall plan works. The weather is truly complex — rules partly unknown, all interconnected, no prediction possible, so you need agile / SCRUM. Complex systems are agent-based, non-linear (a tiny nudge → a wildly different result), emergent, open, self-organised and self-regulating (no boss in charge) and path-dependent (their history matters, not just the present). Two cousins live here too: a CAS (Complex Adaptive System) that learns and rewrites itself (a stock exchange, a human brain), and a MAS (Multi-Agent System) — many small agents with no overarching controller, each one broadcasting when it grabs a job so no one doubles up (think ant colony). And when computer scientists must measure complexity, they count resources: compute time, storage, chip size, number of parallel chips.

To wire Robi sensibly, Cy ties his parts together with string until they form a spider web — its degree of connectivity is just the number of nodes plus the number of causal connections (measured by the clustering coefficient, computed exactly, no rounding, where a two-way link counts as 2). One ironclad rule: parts in the same unit may talk, but parts in different units on the same level must not — if they secretly do, you drew the border in the wrong place, and that means wrong responsibilities later.

Robi is now far too tangled to sketch on a napkin, so you simulate his whole behaviour on a computer and run it a thousand times. That's System Dynamics — in two styles: qualitative, which maps the feedback loops (reinforcing = positive coupling, balancing = negative) for a quick overview, and quantitative, which crunches real numbers across many runs to expose non-linear effects. Mid-simulation a butterfly flaps, the whole run goes wild, and Cy whispers the name: Chaos Theory — non-linear systems where a tiny change explodes into a huge, unpredictable one (turbulent gas, the internet).

Enough analysis — time to actually make Robi. An artist with a paintbrush appears and turns the requirements into something real: plans, drawings, models, code. That's Design: translating requirements (true to the purpose) into a technical solution. But the artist keeps glancing nervously at the factory, because design, integration and production can't be cleanly separated — they tug on each other. Build prototypes and test them early, Cy insists, and never forget the factory's limits: ignore them and you'll draw something impossible to make (a ball sealed inside a ball) or too expensive to make (every wire in solid gold).

Cy zooms all the way out to the whole company, where every process falls in one of three lanes: the core lane (value-adding business with customers and suppliers), the management lane (steering) and the support lane (helpers). She traces BMW's core lane like a relay: ItO (Idea→Offer) → OtO (Offer→Order) → OtD (Order→Delivery) → DtCC (Delivery→Customer-Care). The bit where requirements become a real technical solution — that's the ItO, and it's exactly your job.

Finally Cy reveals the big gear-loop that engineers Robi from start to finish, the discipline called System Engineering: an interdisciplinary mix of engineering and management that designs, integrates and manages complex systems across their whole life cycle, written down in the standard ISO/IEC 15288. Five gears turn in order — (1) Requirement Management (analyse the problem from Customer, Law, Lessons-Learned), (2) Ideation (find functions, draw the architecture, break system→subsystem→component, design interfaces), (3) Design/Realization (model, simulate, build into real HW/E-E/SW — the core engineering work), (4) Test, Verification & Validation (even the “test of the test”; prove by measurement that every requirement is met), (5) Release (sign-off and take accountability to the customer) — and two giant arms hug the entire loop the whole time: Risk Management ☢️ (e.g. FMEA, deviation management) and Change Management 🔁 (version control when requirements shift). Skip all this, Cy warns, and you get the famous tree-swing cartoon, where every department builds something different from what the customer asked for.

A runner crouches at a start line, sweating, because modern CPS projects are brutal: globalization (more suppliers, more law, fuzzy responsibilities), a diversity of interfaces that all need consistent control, more product variants (a VW Golf has ~425 billion wiring-harness combinations → you survive only with reusable component kits), a punishing quality–cost–time trade-off, distributed sensors that need sensor fusion (camera + lidar), systems hard to verify and hard to secure (new attack points everywhere). Cy poses the future's hardest question: when an autonomous robot-car crashes, a whole crowd of suspects lines up — owner, “driver”, sensor maker, AI trainer, dataset supplier… — and nobody can say who's to blame. Mastering that complexity is the job ahead.

So she hands you three power-tools for the road. MBSE = the System Wheel plus a living model everyone shares, holding all system info independent of any one trade, in a common language (UML / SysML), giving traceability and the power to foresee a change's impact. MDSD = a vending machine that spits finished code, tests and docs straight from a diagram (Simulink → C), with no hand-coding. And Digital Engineering (defined by INCOSE, and GfSE in Germany) = a ghost-twin of Robi you watch perform live and poke virtually, powered by MBSE, AI, CAD/CAE, additive manufacturing and VR/AR. With those, your napkin idea is ready to become a real robot — and you've quietly learned the entire chapter.

The moral (your essay close): complexity keeps rising — more components, a VUCA world, everything interconnected — so engineers must build whole systems, not lone parts, and chase the holistic optimum instead of a local one. System = WHAT, Design = HOW, then engineer the whole thing.

01System Design = two halves

🧒 Story
Before you touch a screwdriver, Cy splits the word. System Design = “the System” (its purpose) + “the Design” (how you build it). Robi's purpose is “clean the floor”; the design is the wiring diagram and code that make it happen.
📝 Write in exam
  • System Design consists of two parts: the System (purpose) and the Design (how).
🧠
System = WHAT · Design = HOW. Two words, two halves of the whole course.

02What is a system?

🧒 Story
Cy hoots, “What is a system?” Look at Robi: lots of different parts all chasing one purpose, and you can draw a fence separating it from the world. If the parts were all the same with no shared purpose, it's just a pile (accumulation), not a system.
📝 Write in exam — definition
  • A system = a group of associated components that work together for the same purpose and that can be bordered from the environment.
  • Components have different characteristics — otherwise it's an accumulation, not a system.
  • Systems are clearly bordered; sometimes partial overlaps at interfaces.
  • Natural (no human): Galaxy (life) · Leaf (photosynthesis) · Lung (breathing).
  • Artificial (man-made): Piano (sound) · Airplane (fly) · Robot (expressions); also search engine, sports team, organization.
🧠
Different parts · one purpose · a fence around it. No fence or all-same-parts = not a system.

03System Classification

🧒 Story
Cy shows a coral-reef tank — living coral + machine pumps. Systems sort into 3 bins, man-made ones into 3 more, and each is tagged by how open its doors are.
📝 Write in exam
  • Types of systems: Natural · Artificial · Biotechnical.
  • Man-made types: Technical (machines, tools) · Social (family, groups, work) · Sociotechnical (technical + social influencing each other; e.g. information/work system).
  • By exchange with environment: Open (full interfaces — info/material/energy) · Partly open (small pre-defined data, e.g. OBD in a car) · Isolated (nothing exchanged).
  • Parts can differ: a robot's tracking = isolated, its map → open Bluetooth.
🧠
3 doors on Robi → wide-Open · mail-slot (Partly, “OBD”) · welded-shut (Isolated).

04Cyber, Cybernetics & CPS

🧒 Story
Why the “Cyber” in Cyber-Physical? Cyber = short for cybernetics = controlling machines using feedback — like Robi's brain asking “am I where I should be?” and correcting. Cy draws the control loop: Target − Current = a gap → Controller → Actuator → the world reacts → Sensor reads it → loop again.
📝 Write in exam
  • Cyber = short form of cybernetics. Cybernetics = control of machines incl. feedback of sense organs (analogy to lifeforms) and social interactions (communication).
  • Control loop (in order): Target value → difference (target − current) → Controller → Command → Actuator → Action → System/Environment → Reaction → Sensor → Current value → fed back (−).
  • “Cyber” umbrella term: Cybersecurity, Cyberspace, Cyberwarfare, Cybercrime, Cyber-attack, Cyborgs (organic + biomechatronic crossover).
🧠
Cyber = feedback brain. The loop is a circle: gap → fix → measure → repeat.

04aMechatronic vs CPS

🧒 Story
A lone washing machine is mechatronic — software + hardware, sealed, standalone. Now let the washer text the solar panels to run when the sun shines — that networked smart-home is a CPS.
📝 Write in exam
  • Mechatronic system = software + hardware (mechanics & electronics); standalone/closed. E.g. washing machine.
  • CPS = intercommunicating software (esp. embedded, via internet = cyber) + hardware (= physical); for large-scale, distributed networks (IoT). E.g. home automation linking washer + photovoltaic.
  • No sharp deviation between mechatronic and CPS.
  • Applied to this lecture: physical (sensors, actuators, PCB & wires) + cyber (processing unit, AI, general software) → a CPS.
🧠
Mechatronic = lonely washer. CPS = washer that texts the solar panels.

05System Wheel

🧒 Story
Cy spins a bicycle wheel — the course's master diagram. The hub is Robi's “DNA” datasheet (performance, weight, cost…). Spokes are extra properties (safety, EMC, thermal…). The rim is painted in 3 colours = the building trades: mechanics, E/E, software. The customer stands at the edge. (Full detail in Requirement Management.)
📝 Write in exam
  • Rings (inside→out): core “DNA” properties (datasheet) → extended properties (acoustics, efficiency, data/security, design-for-production/service, safety, EMC, sustainability, reliability, thermal, volumetric) → realization domain (mechanics / E/E / software) → customer.
  • Customer raises requirements on system level.
🧠
Wheel = Hub (DNA) · Spokes (properties) · 3-colour Rim (mech/E-E/SW) · Customer at the edge.

06System Structure (SIPOC)

🧒 Story
Every system, says Cy, is secretly a conveyor belt: something comes in, gets processed, goes out. That's SIPOC. For Robi: a Sensor feels → a wire carries → the Brain/Logic decides → a wire carries → a Motor acts.
📝 Write in exam
  • Every process = SIPOC (Supplier · Input · Process · Output · Customer).
  • High-level structure: Sensor → Connector → Logic → Connector → Actuator/Visualizer.
  • Connector = PCB / wire / wireless · Logic = software / IC / state machine / AI · Actuator/Visualizer = motor / valve / HMI / database.
  • ChatGPT: prompt page → server → AI magic → browser → output field.
  • Sorting machine: colour sensor 0–5 V → TXT controller → threshold picks pusher 1/2/3 → motor driver → pusher fires.
🧠
“See It Process Output Customer” = Sensor → Connector → Logic → Connector → Actuator. A conveyor belt, always.

07System Levels & Tiers

🧒 Story
Pull Robi apart like Russian nesting dolls: the big Supersystem holds Systems, which hold Sub-systems, which hold Components. A higher level is just the sum of the dolls inside.
📝 Write in exam
  • Levels (top→bottom): Supersystem (4, rare) → System (3) → Sub-system (2) → Component (1).
  • A higher level = the sum of the elements below; lower-level count is variable but always > 1.
  • Supersystem & Sub-system optional; System & Component always exist.
  • Same element, different level by viewpoint: a car = system for the car-maker, but the screw = system for the screw supplier.
  • In industry, supplier levels = “tiers” (manufacturer → tier-1 → tier-2 → tier-3 → …).
🧠
Nesting dolls. System + Component are the two that MUST exist; Super/Sub are optional. Your level depends on who holds the doll (tier).

08Components

🧒 Story
Tip Robi out onto the bench — a pile of bricks. Some you can touch (hardware, software, liquids), some you can't (talents, roles, ideas). Each brick is fenced off and does its own job.
📝 Write in exam
  • Components = the basic elements of a system.
  • Real (material): Hardware (electronics/mechanics/hydraulics) · Software (functions/interfaces) · Material/consumables (chemicals/liquids).
  • Immaterial: Talents & Roles · Ideas.
  • Components are clearly bordered and have different functions/tasks.
🧠
Brick pile — touchable bricks (HW/SW/liquids) + invisible bricks (people/ideas).

09Emergence

🧒 Story
Drop one sulphur crumb — colourless. Pour a pile — it glows yellow. The colour belongs to the group, not the crumb. That surprise extra property = emergence, “1+1=3”. But if something only seems magic because we're ignorant, that's NOT emergence — it vanishes once science catches up.
📝 Write in exam
  • Emergence = a system has other properties than the simple sum of its components (“1+1=3”).
  • Examples: single sulphur atom = no colour, many = yellow; single molecule = no pressure, gas = pressure.
  • Ignorance is NOT emergence — effects merely unknown (low state-of-the-art) that disappear as knowledge evolves.
🧠
Glowing sulphur pile. The property lives in the crowd, not the crumb. Don't-know ≠ emergence.

10Complexity & VUCA

🧒 Story
A storm cloud rolls over the workshop — the modern world is VUCA: a Very Ugly Chaotic Atmosphere. Then Cy plays a game: a subway map vs the weather — which is complex? The map is only complicated (hard but all rules known); the weather is truly complex (rules unknown, all tangled).
📝 Write in exam
  • VUCA: Volatile (discontinuity, high speed, big swings) · Uncertain (unclear outcomes, low predictability) · Complex (many layers, fuzzy boundaries) · Ambiguous (no simple description, multiple interpretations).
  • Complexity is set by the amount, order & interaction of components; complex systems can't be broken into easy problems.
  • Properties of complex systems: Agent-based · Non-linear · Emergence · Open · Self-organised · Self-regulating · Path-dependencies (history matters).
  • CAS = changes itself (stock exchange, brain). MAS/ABM = self-organised agents, no boss, broadcast jobs (ant colony, DAI).
  • Measure complexity (IT): Computational Complexity Theory via resource demand (time, storage, chip size, # parallel chips).
🧠
VUCA = the storm. Complicated = subway map (waterfall). Complex = weather (SCRUM).

11Degree of Connectivity

🧒 Story
Tie Robi's parts together with string until they form a spider web. The more knots and threads, the higher the connectivity. Rule: parts in the same unit may talk; parts in different units on the same level must NOT — if they do, you drew the border wrong (→ wrong responsibilities!).
📝 Write in exam
  • Same-level, same-unit elements may interact & affect each other.
  • No interaction between same-level elements in different units — else the border is wrong → risk of wrong responsibilities.
  • Interactions swap information / material / energy, are interdependent, not unidirectional.
  • Degree of connectivity = (amount of different nodes) + (amount of causal connections).
  • Measured by the clustering coefficient (exact, no rounding; a bidirectional link = 2).
🧠
Spider web — count knots + threads; a two-way thread = 2. Cross-unit threads = a wiring mistake.

12System Dynamics & Chaos Theory

🧒 Story
You can't sketch Robi's full behaviour on a napkin — too tangled. So you simulate the whole system on a computer and run it 1000 times. That's System Dynamics. Then a butterfly flaps, the simulation goes wild and unpredictable — Chaos Theory.
📝 Write in exam
  • System Dynamics = model & simulate the whole system (complex systems can't be described easily/linearly). Variants: qualitative & quantitative.
  • Qualitative — find feedback loops: reinforcing (positive coupling) & balancing (negative coupling); easier; good overview.
  • Quantitative — model & simulate; real numbers; many runs; flow charts/process views; finds non-linear effects.
  • Chaos Theory = non-linear dynamic systems; “butterfly effect” — small change → big effect, prediction impossible (turbulent gas, internet).
🧠
Simulate 1000× = System Dynamics (Qualitative = arrows/loops, Quantitative = numbers). Butterfly = Chaos.

13Design

🧒 Story
Now an artist with a paintbrush turns Robi's requirements into something real — drawings, models, code. But the artist can't ignore the factory: design, integration and production all pull on each other. A design nobody can manufacture (a ball inside a ball, or all-gold wires) is useless.
📝 Write in exam
  • Design = translating requirements (respecting the system purpose) into a (technical) solution.
  • Deliverables: Plan · Drawings/schematics · Models · Software code · …
  • Design, integration & production can't be strictly divided — they influence each other; build prototypes and test upfront.
  • Without production know-how → risk of a non-producible (ball-in-ball) or non-commercial (all-gold wires) part.
🧠
Paintbrush turns requirements → drawings/models/code; but the factory is always watching.

14System Design Process

🧒 Story
Zoom out to the whole company factory. Every process is one of 3 lanes: the core lane (money made with customers/suppliers), the management lane (steering), the support lane (helpers). BMW's core lane reads like a relay: Idea → Offer → Order → Delivery → Customer-Care.
📝 Write in exam
  • 3 process classes: Core (business with customers & suppliers; value-adding) · Management · Support. Flow: customer requirement → core process → customer satisfaction.
  • BMW core process: ItO (Idea→Offer) · OtO (Offer→Order) · OtD (Order→Delivery) · DtCC (Delivery→Customer-Care).
  • ItO = development from requirement → ideas/concepts → implementation; purchasing procures; after test & validation + industrialisation → release for production. Aim: optimal product, all requirements met.
🧠
3 lanes = Core · Management · Support. BMW relay: I-O-O-D → Idea, Offer, Order, Delivery (then Care).

15System Engineering — the 7 steps ⭐

🧒 Story
Here's the big gear-loop that actually builds Robi. Five steps run in order — Requirements → Ideation → Design → Test → Release — and two more, Risk ☢️ and Change 🔁, hug the whole loop the entire time. Defined by a standard, ISO 15288.
📝 Write in exam — SE is
  • an interdisciplinary field of engineering + engineering management;
  • design, integrate & manage complex systems over their life cycle; defined in ISO/IEC 15288.
📝 The steps (in order)
  • 1. Requirement Management — analyse problem & requests (Customer, Law, LeLe); break into requirements; define system requirements.
  • 2. Ideation / Concept — creative search for ideas (maybe research!); define functions; create system architecture; break (super)system → subsystem → component; break requirements into levels; interface design.
  • 3. Design / Realization — model, simulate, analyse; implement into HW/E-E/SW; integrate levels; document. → core engineering work.
  • 4. Test & Verification & Validation — test design, the “test of the test”, planning, execution (virtual + HW). → proof by measurement every requirement is met.
  • 5. Release — sign off; release to production; take over accountability to the customer.
  • Risk Management (surrounding) — check risks to product/time/cost/safety (e.g. FMEA); deviation management.
  • Change Management (surrounding) — update on changing requirements (new law, customer change of mind, LeLe/field issues); version control.
🧠
Gear-loop: R-I-D-T-R (Requirements, Ideation, Design, Test, Release) wrapped by Risk ☢️ + Change 🔁. Standard = 15288. Without it → the “tree-swing” cartoon.

16System Engineering — Challenges

🧒 Story
A runner at the start line is sweating because modern CPS projects are brutal: the world went global, everything connects to everything, customers want endless variants, and you can't even prove the thing is safe.
📝 Write in exam
  • Globalization → many suppliers; rising legal specs; unclear responsibilities (fuzzy boundaries).
  • Interdisciplinary work mandatory; diverse interfaces → consistent control of all interfaces.
  • Complex requirements → more dynamics; more variants → component kits (reusable units). (VW Golf ≈ 425 billion wiring-harness combos.)
  • Reduced cycle time → hard quality–cost–time trade-off (VUCA).
  • Distributed sensor network → sensor fusion (camera + lidar). Communication handling (when? which path?). Information gathering (lots of changing data).
  • Hard to verify (can't stop it, who's root cause, hard to debug, AI changes). Safety (trust network data; new attack points).
🧠
Sweating runner. Everything got bigger, more connected, more varied, harder to verify & secure. “Who crashed the robot-car?” = nobody can tell → the future challenge.

17MBSE, MDSD & Digital Engineering

🧒 Story
Finally, three power-tools. MBSE = the System Wheel with a working model so every discipline reads the same picture. MDSD = a vending machine that spits out finished code/tests/docs straight from a diagram (Simulink → C). Digital Engineering = a ghost-twin of Robi you can watch and poke live.
📝 Write in exam
  • MBSE (Model-Based Systems Engineering) = System Wheel + a model; cross-discipline communication; all info independent of realization domains; needs a harmonized modelling language. Languages: UML & SysML.
  • MBSE benefits: visualization · architectures/dependencies → traceability · automated tests · different views · foreseeable change impact · direct code generation (MDSD).
  • MDSD / MDD = auto-generate functional software, tests, documentation from the model without coding (Simulink → C). Pro: abstraction, speed, fitting test cases. Con: more initial effort, tricky manual-code integration, harder troubleshooting.
  • Digital Engineering = successor of MBSE (INCOSE / GfSE); uses “digital twins” (live + virtual interaction); uses MBSE · AI · CAD/CAE · additive manufacturing · VR/AR.
🧠
MBSE = shared model · MDSD = vending machine (diagram → code) · Digital Engineering = ghost-twin. Languages = UML/SysML; councils = INCOSE/GfSE.

Q1Define a “system” and distinguish a mechatronic system from a cyber-physical system. Give one example of each.
Type: definitions · ~8 marks
✓ Model answer

A system is a group of associated components that work together for the same purpose and that can be bordered from the environment. The components have different characteristics — otherwise it would be a mere accumulation. Borders are clear, though interfaces may partly overlap.

  • Mechatronic system: a merge of software + hardware (mechanics & electronics); can be a standalone, closed system. Example: a washing machine.
  • Cyber-Physical System (CPS): a merge of intercommunicating software (esp. embedded, via the internet = cyber) + hardware (= physical); suited to large-scale, distributed networks (IoT). Example: home automation linking the washing machine with the photovoltaic system.

There is no sharp deviation between the two. Applied to this lecture a system = physical (sensors, actuators, PCB & wires) + cyber (processing unit, AI, software).

Q2Explain the difference between a “complicated” and a “complex” system, define VUCA, and name what makes a system complex (its properties).
Type: structured / theory · ~10 marks
✓ Model answer

Complicated systems have all elements, rules and dependencies known; they need much effort but are predictable and can be mastered by training/knowledge → managed with a waterfall model (e.g. a subway grid plan). Complex systems have (partly) unknown elements, rules and dependencies; they are not predictable and cannot be “removed” → managed with agile / SCRUM (e.g. the weather).

VUCA = Volatile (discontinuity, big fluctuations), Uncertain (unclear outcomes, low predictability), Complex (many layers, fuzzy boundaries), Ambiguous (no simple description).

Properties of complex systems: agent-based, non-linear, emergence, open, self-organised, self-regulating, and path-dependencies (behaviour depends on history, not just the current state).

Q3You are building Robi, a cleaning robot. (a) Draw/describe its high-level system structure (SIPOC). (b) Name the steps of the System Engineering process. (c) Give two challenges of engineering such a CPS.
Type: application · ~9 marks
✓ Model answer

(a) Every system follows the SIPOC chain: Sensor → Connector → Logic → Connector → Actuator/Visualizer. For Robi: a dirt/obstacle sensor → a wire (connector) → the logic (software/AI on a processing unit) → a wire → the drive motor/brush (actuator).

(b) System Engineering (ISO/IEC 15288) runs: Requirement Management → Ideation/Concept → Design/Realization → Test & Verification & Validation → Release, with Risk Management and Change Management surrounding the whole loop.

(c) Any two of: increasing interdisciplinary work & diverse interfaces (consistent control needed); many product variants; distributed sensor network needing sensor fusion; hard to verify (can't stop the whole system, AI keeps changing); safety (untrusted network data, new attack points); unclear accountability (e.g. who is liable when an autonomous car crashes).

Bonus: 10 rapid-fire short answers
  1. System Design = System (purpose) + Design (how).
  2. Emergence = “1+1=3” (sulphur pile is yellow); ignorance ≠ emergence.
  3. System levels: Supersystem · System · Sub-system · Component (System & Component always exist).
  4. Cybernetics = control of machines via feedback (sense organs + social).
  5. Open / Partly-open / Isolated — partly-open example = OBD in a car.
  6. Connectivity = nodes + connections; bidirectional link counts as 2 (clustering coefficient).
  7. System Dynamics: Qualitative (loops) vs Quantitative (real numbers).
  8. Chaos theory = non-linear, butterfly effect (turbulent gas, internet).
  9. BMW core process: ItO · OtO · OtD · DtCC.
  10. MDSD = auto-generate code/tests/docs from the model (Simulink → C).
