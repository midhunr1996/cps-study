📖 The Full Story — “The Wish That Had to Be Caught Exactly”

The whole chapter as one connected tale. Don't try to memorize it — just read it like a story and let it run as a film in your head. Because each thing happens for a reason, the concepts stick by themselves. Exam words are in bold; the table at the end maps every beat to the point you write in the exam.

A kid named Mira pushes open the door of a robot shop; a bell jingles, and she announces: “I want a robot pet!” Behind the counter sits Pip, a bright green parrot, whose entire job is to repeat the wish back — perfectly. Because in the workshop at the back, dozens of builders will build exactly what Pip squawks, word for word. Mishear one syllable and they build the wrong robot. So Pip leans in, because a wish is not yet a buildable order. His whole task is to turn Mira's fuzzy human wish into exact, written, testable cards the workshop can obey — and keep them straight for the robot's entire life.

First Pip writes the idea of his trade on the wall: a requirement is an accurately phrased development target for one property, already accounting for every relevant condition (will the pet live outdoors? must it be silent at night?). One robot pet, he warns, needs hundreds to tens of thousands of these cards — and the count only grows (an autonomous car gains more every year). Serious shops follow rulebooks — ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148, SQuaRE (ISO 25000), maturity models like CMMI and SPICE — and it all lives inside the ItO — Idea to Offer, the journey from a customer's wish to a finished product.

Why be this fussy? Pip points at a cartoon pinned by the till: the famous tree-swing. The customer described a swing, sales drew another, the engineer a third, the workshop built a fourth, and what the customer truly needed was a fifth — all from the same few words. That chaos is exactly why Pip exists. Requirement Management finds every requirement from supersystem down to component level and keeps them alive across the whole lifecycle; it is the enabler of customer satisfaction, of one common understanding between customer and supplier, and of avoiding safety/field disasters and costly late changes. Set the targets wrong, Pip says gravely, and the whole product is wrong. His three eternal enemies: an unclear definition, an incomplete set (the missing card you find too late), and an inaccurately interpreted card.

To keep customer and workshop honest, Pip carries two binders. Mira hands him the red WISH binder — the PRD (Lastenheft) — the sum of everything she wants (the “what”), used in the nomination phase to ask suppliers for offers. The workshop reads it and writes back the green PROMISE binder — the Implementation Spec (Pflichtenheft) — how they'll build it and with what (technical concept, resources, plan), where every PRD requirement must be answered by at least one deliverable. The day Mira signs the green binder, building begins. Red = the customer's Request; green = the supplier's Guarantee.

The work itself runs as six scenes, always in order, and Pip acts them out. Scene one — Collect: he grabs a basket and scoops up wishes — from outside first (the customer before everything, then law, standards, cultural and social expectations) and from inside the shop (the old Lessons-Learned notebook, house standards, the kit of motors he's allowed, company strategy). He notices the more people he interviews, the less new information each one adds. He captures each demand as a use case: “As a child, I want a pet that follows me, so that I'm never lonely.” That LeLe notebook is sacred: read at kick-off, updated throughout, and at the closing workshop the team bakes new wisdom into the standard — obeying the golden rule that a fix is never just the negation of the problem. “We tested too late” must become “start the test at least two weeks before release, written into the project plan,” not “don't be late.”

Scene two — Assess: Pip can't build everything, so he sets a judge's scale and weighs each wish three ways — is it relevant to this product, how important is it, what kind is it? The celebrity judge is Mr. Kano, who sorts every feature onto one of five shelves: the bread shelf (basic/must — invisible when present, fury when missing), the dial (performance/should — more is happier), the surprise gift (delighter/could — unexpected wow), the grey box (indifferent — nobody cares), and the stink bomb (reverse/don't — having it makes things worse). The shelves shift by customer, by price, and over time (today's delighter is tomorrow's bread). To find the shelf, Pip asks a functional and a dysfunctional question as a pair and reads the class from the combination. He also files each wish as Product / Process / External (What / How / Rest) and as either Functional or a non-functional requirement (NFR) — the “how well,” like 99.9% uptime.

Scene three — Develop: Pip writes each survivor as a tiny, perfect index card — one idea per card, active voice, written so a robot could obey it. A good card is complete, correct, SMART, testable, linked, atomic, free of weak words (“roughly”, “very”) and free of “should/must.” Each carries a passport: unique ID, who asked, who wrote it, who'll use it, its fit-criterion (test), priority, version, status. The SMART brain wears a smartwatch: Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Reasonable · Time-bound. “Specific” matters because language lies — “I saw the man on the mountain with the telescope” gets drawn five ways, so you pin it down with IF–THEN logic. But don't over-specify: a firm asked to stop bicycles being damaged in shipping wasn't told how, so someone shipped each bike in a box printed to look like a TV — damage plummeted. Specify the problem, not the solution. “Testable” means measurable and affordable to test (don't crash ten real cars to check one card). And every card must be linked — recall the bridge built by two countries whose sea-level zeros differed, meeting mid-river with a 28 cm step, then “fixed” the wrong way and doubled.

Scene four — Tier down: Pip carries the cards down a ladder — supersystem → system → sub-system → component — handing each floor an order it can actually build and own. The loudspeaker maker refuses to promise the sound inside the whole car — he never built the room! At every door stands a bouncer: does this card fit? In. If not — can I tweak it? Yes → modify; no → toss (with a reason). Only promise what you can build. Scenes five & six — Verify and Changes: Pip doesn't run the tests himself; he simply ticks each card off as the workshop proves it built right (real testing belongs to the Test chapter), and when anyone changes their mind he chases that change through every linked card, handing the heavy lifting to Change Management.

To forget no property of Mira's pet, Pip spins the System Wheel like a wagon wheel: a hub of the three trades (mechanics, E/E, software), an inner ring of the seven “DNA” datasheet properties, and an outer ring of ten extended properties (safety, EMC, thermal, sustainability…). Each property gets a property engineer — the voice of the customer for that one property — who optimises across the whole system, not a single component. One last warning: Pip could polish cards forever and never let the workshop start — the trap of analysis paralysis. Don't freeze; start building. And because thousands of cards can't live in Word or Excel, real shops use tools (DOORS, Codebeamer, Jama), and when two tools — or customer and supplier — must talk, they share one universal XML adapter, ReqIF (Requirement Interchange Format, kept by ProSTEP iViP).

The moral (your essay close): RM is the most important step in system design — it runs in six phases, focuses on properties not solutions, carries the red PRD and green Implementation Spec, and remembers that if the targets are wrong the product is wrong — but don't drown in analysis; start realizing.

01What is a requirement?

🧒 Story
Mira says “robot pet.” That's a wish, not a buildable order. Pip's job is to turn it into a requirement: an exact written target for one property that already accounts for all conditions (outdoors? quiet at night?). One robot needs hundreds to tens of thousands of these cards.
📝 Write in exam — Definition
  • Requirement = an accurately phrased development target for a property of the system/component, considering all relevant conditions.
  • Amount: several hundred to (ten-)thousands (grows over time, e.g. autonomous cars).
  • Defined in standards: ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 (software req. engineering), ISO/IEC 25000 (SQuaRE); part of maturity models CMMI, SPICE (ISO/IEC 15504), ISO/IEC 12207.
  • Lives inside ItO – Idea to Offer: customer requirement → ItO → offer → product (purchasing procures in parallel; after test/validation & industrialization → release).
🧠
A requirement is one exact card for one property — not a vague wish. Software rulebook number to anchor: 29148.

02Why do we need RM? (the tree-swing)

🧒 Story
The famous tree-swing cartoon: the customer explains a swing, sales describes another, the designer draws a third, the workshop builds a fourth — and what the customer really needed was something else entirely. That mess is exactly what Pip prevents.
📝 Write in exam — Why RM
  • RM identifies the necessary requirements from (super)system level down to component level and maintains them over the whole lifecycle.
  • RM is the enabler to: increase customer satisfaction · get a common understanding · avoid safety/field (quality) issues · avoid late changes & cost impacts · feed LeLe.
  • ➡ RM is the essential discipline in systems design — if the targets are set wrong, the whole product is wrong!
  • Challenges (3): clear & distinct definition · completeness (missing reqs found too late) · inaccurate interpretation. → LeLe is important!
🧠
Same words, five different swings. RM = one common picture.

03The two binders (Core Documents)

🧒 Story
Pip carries two binders. Mira hands over the 📕 red WISH binder (PRD) = everything she wants. The workshop reads it and writes back the 📗 green PROMISE binder (Implementation Spec) = exactly how and with what. When Mira signs the green binder, building begins.
📝 Write in exam — PRD (Lastenheft)
  • Sum of all requirements of a commercial customer (not necessarily the end user); standards DIN 69901-5 / IEEE SRS 29148.
  • Used in the nomination phase to request technical & commercial offers from suppliers.
  • Content: intro · current state · target (main reqs) · interfaces · functional · NFR · system architecture · expected scope of delivery · acceptance criteria.
  • Internal dev → detailed & tiered down. External dev → generic (property level) to avoid specifying the solution.
📝 Write in exam — Implementation Spec (Pflichtenheft)
  • The supplier's answer to the customer's PRD: the “How” (technical concept) and the “With what” (resources, project plan).
  • Every PRD requirement must be covered by ≥1 deliverable. Customer acceptance → development starts.
🧠
Red = Request (customer, “what”) · Green = Guarantee (supplier, “how/with what”). Lastenheft = load you carry → Pflichtenheft = duty you accept.

04The 6-phase process (the spine)

🧒 Story
This is the movie again — Pip's six scenes. Different rulebooks describe it (IEEE SWEBOK, CMMI, IIBA/BABOK, Volere, IREB), but the lecture uses one variant with two freeze points: PRD freeze and Implementation-spec freeze. In big projects a requirement manager steers it.
📝 Write in exam
  • 6 phases: (1) Collect → (2) Assess → (3) Develop → (4) Tier down → (5) Validation/Verification → (6) Changes.
  • Cascade of who: Customer (request) → Property Engineer (1→x system properties) → Developer (x→y component requirements).
🧠
basket → scale → index cards → ladder → checklist → catch the changes.

05(1) COLLECT — fill the basket

🧒 Story
Pip scoops up wishes from outside (Mira first!, plus law and rules) and from inside the shop (old notebooks, house rules). But careful — interviewing more and more people gives less and less new info (diminishing returns).
📝 Write in exam
  • External requests: !!!Customer!!! (the prior source!) · Law · Standards & guidelines · Cultural · Political · Social expectations.
  • Internal requests: LeLe · company standards · existing tool kits · company strategy · CI/design · business-dept restrictions.
  • Use cases capture demand: “As [role] / I would like to [function] / To [reason]”; named after the goal.
🧠
Customer first, then the world outside, then your own shop. And fewer secrets per extra interviewee.

05aLeLe — Lessons Learned

🧒 Story
At the kick-off, the team reads the old notebook so they don't repeat last project's mistakes. They keep updating it, and at the end hold a workshop to write the new wisdom into the standard.
📝 Write in exam
  • Use prior-project knowledge at the start (kick-off) → constantly update during → end workshop transfers “what went well / what to improve” into the new standard. Covers technical, PM, commercial knowledge; document in software.
  • Golden rule: the solution is NOT the negation of the problem. “We started the test too late” → ❌ “don't start so late” → ✅ “start the test ≥2 weeks before release and anchor it in the project plan.” (Same for positive feedback.)

05bStandards & “who is the customer?”

📝 Write in exam
  • Regional: TGL (DDR) · DIN (German) · GB/GB-T (China). International: EN (European) · ISO (mechanics) · IEC (electrical/electronics). Industry: IATF (automotive) · VDE (electrotechnics).
  • “Who is the customer?” pitfall: wrong requirements come from misunderstanding who the real customer/user is (parents choosing for the child).

06(2) ASSESS — the judge's scale & Kano

🧒 Story
Pip can't build everything, so he judges each wish three ways: is it relevant here? how important is it? what kind is it? The most famous judge on the scale is Mr. Kano, who sorts every feature onto one of 5 shelves by how it moves Mira's happiness.
📝 Write in exam — assess by
  • Relevance (relevant for this product? e.g. not sold in Japan → ignore Japanese law) · Priority (which wins if not all fit, e.g. airbag > turn signal; depends on company strategy) · Classification.
  • Design strategies driving priority: Design for Customer (Design Thinking) · Costs · Quality/Reliability · Production/Manufacturing (DFM) · Service.
📝 Write in exam — Kano model (Prof. Noriaki Kano)
  • Basic (must) — bread: invisible if present, furious if missing; no competitor differentiation. (call-centre greeting)
  • Performance (should) — dial: more = happier. (hotline waiting time)
  • Delighter (could) — surprise gift: unexpected → wow; a little gives a lot. (live ticket-status tracking)
  • Indifferent — grey box: present or not, nobody cares. (hold-music song)
  • Reverse (don't) — stink bomb: its presence lowers satisfaction. (jargon in a hotline call)
  • Classification shifts by customer, by price/business model, and over time (delighters become basics).
  • Survey: standardised (remove interviewer impact) + demographics; ask a functional + dysfunctional pair.
🧠
Order of happiness: Bread → Dial → Gift → Grey box → Stink bomb. (Car: must=it drives · should=horsepower · could=launch control · indifferent=underfloor-screw colour · reverse=new rust after a wash.)

06aClassification of requests

📝 Write in exam
  • By belonging: Product (What) [SE focus] · Process (How) · External (Rest).
  • By function: Functional (prints my file) vs NFR (24/7, 99.9% uptime — not a feature but still expected).
  • Function attributes: inputs/outputs · value range/dimensions · tolerance · how often executed · how fast (throughput time).

07(3) DEVELOP — write the perfect index card

🧒 Story
Now Pip writes each requirement as a tiny, perfect card. Rule of thumb: one idea per card, written like a robot could obey it.
📝 Write in exam — a good requirement is…
  • Complete (no supplier assumptions) · correct · SMART · testable · linked · necessary (no “nice-to-haves”).
  • Short · atomic (one req/sentence) · active voice (“The motor rotates the shaft” ✅, not “is driven by” ❌) · no weak words (“really/very/roughly” — tools ReQualize, DESIRe) · avoid “should/must”.
  • Understandable · traceable · unambiguous & consistent · feasible · equipped with properties.
📝 Properties of a requirement
  • Unique ID · description · rationale/target · originator · creator · user · fit criterion (testable) · priority · dependencies/interfaces · conflicts · release/maturity · linked documents · comments · version · change history · status.
🧠
One card, one idea, active voice, no fuzzy words — and give each card a passport (ID, who, why, test, version, status).

07aSMART

🧒 Story
Specific: “I saw the man on the mountain with the telescope” — five people draw five different pictures → fix with IF–THEN–ELSE code. Not too specific: a company asked to cut a bicycle's shipping damage (not “improve the box”) → result: a box that looks like a TV, far fewer damages. Specify the problem, not the solution.
📝 Write in exam
  • SMART = Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Reasonable · Time-Bound.
  • Natural language is ambiguous → use mathematical/IF-THEN descriptions and exclude variants.
🧠
Brain in a smart-watch. + telescope = be specific / bicycle-in-a-TV-box = don't over-specify.

07bTestable & writing guideline

📝 Write in exam
  • To be testable a requirement must be specific, detailed, affordable to test, linked to a maturity level. (“The charger needs to be efficient” = untestable — 75%? 80%?)
  • Verification must be affordable (don't crash 10 real cars to check “mute button accessible after a crash”).
Show the writing guideline (Bad → Better)
GuidelineBadBetter
Positive not negativeThe box is not complicated to open.The box is easy to open.
Avoid “could/should”If possible, the bike should be easy to clean.The bike is easy to clean.
SpecificThe cage is robust.The cage operates normally after 10× dropping from 2 m onto concrete.
What not howThe bag has a handle on the top.The bag has a possibility to carry it.

07cLinked + SCRUM

🧒 Story
Every card holds hands with its neighbours. The 🌉 bridge built from two countries (North-Sea zero vs Mediterranean zero) ended with a 28 cm step — and the fix went the wrong way, doubling it. That's what unlinked requirements do.
📝 Write in exam
  • Link each requirement to: its request · child & mother reqs (hierarchy) · neighbour reqs (mutual impact) · functions & NFR · test items · interfaces.
  • SCRUM (agile, software): Epic → feature request → product backlog — same idea, highly simplified; backlog rows use the role/function/reason format.
🧠
The 28 cm bridge step = always link your requirements.

08The System Wheel

🧒 Story
To not forget any property of Mira's robot, Pip spins a wagon wheel of properties (recap from chapter 1, used here to develop requirements as a “datasheet”).
📝 Write in exam — 3 layers
  • Hub = realization domain: mechanics · E/E · software.
  • Inner ring = core “DNA” (datasheet): performance · consumption · acoustic · costs · reliability · weight · size.
  • Outer ring = 10 extended properties: acoustical behaviour · performance & efficiency · data acquisition/access & security · design for production/service/upgrade · safety (ISO 26262, FuSiKo/HARA) · EMC (emission, immunity, ESD) · sustainability · reliability · thermal behaviour · volumetric behaviour & design (incl. FMK tolerance concept, crash behaviour).
  • Property engineer = the voice of the customer for one property; optimises across the system (not one component); thinks in system functions; the system engineer decides conflicts.
🧠
Wheel = hub (3 domains) + inner ring (7 DNA) + outer ring (10 extended). Property engineer = one property's customer-voice.

09(4) TIER DOWN — carry the cards down the ladder

🧒 Story
Pip walks the cards down from supersystem → system → sub-system → component, handing each floor an order it can actually build and own. A loudspeaker maker won't promise the sound inside the whole car — he didn't build the room! Each receiver must accept, reject, or ask to change the card.
📝 Write in exam
  • Tier down system → component level; the receiver (e.g. component team) must accept / reject / feedback for change and be able to take responsibility.
  • New-requirement flow: new req → inbox/pool → acceptable? yes → add to component reqs → END. No → modification possible? yes → modify (or decline with reason) · no → discard.
  • Hierarchy: Supersystem (4) → System (3) → Sub-system (2) → Component (1).
🧠
A bouncer at each door: fits? in. Doesn't? can I tweak it? yes→modify / no→toss. Only promise what you can build.

10(5) Verify & (6) Changes + Analysis Paralysis

🧒 Story
Pip doesn't run the tests himself — he just ticks them off as the workshop proves each card. When anyone changes their mind, he chases the change through every card. And he must never freeze polishing cards forever instead of letting the build start.
📝 Write in exam
  • (5) Validation/Verification: all reqs must be testable; RM does not verify but tracks that each was confirmed by testing (→ Test & Verification chapter).
  • (6) Changes: track & follow up changes of requests/requirements (→ Change Management chapter).
  • Analysis paralysis: RM is vital, but don't analyse forever to be 100% perfect instead of starting realization.
🧠
RM tracks the ticks, it doesn't do the tests. Changes → Change Management. And don't freeze at the desk — start building.

11Software, ReqIF & Summary

🧒 Story

Q1Describe the requirement-management process. Name its phases, and explain the two core documents (PRD and Implementation Specification) and who is responsible for them.
Type: structured / theory · ~10 marks
✓ Model answer

RM follows a fixed process: (1) Collect requests → (2) Assess requests → (3) Develop requirements → (4) Tier down → (5) Validation/Verification → (6) Follow up of changes, with a PRD freeze and an Implementation-spec freeze; in large projects a requirement manager steers it. RM exists to build the right product (the tree-swing problem) and follows the rule: if the targets are set wrong, the whole product is wrong.

  • PRD (Product Requirements Document / Lastenheft): the customer's sum of all requirements (DIN 69901-5 / IEEE SRS); used in the nomination phase to request offers. Content: current state, target, interfaces, functional + non-functional reqs, architecture, scope of delivery, acceptance criteria.
  • Implementation Specification (Pflichtenheft): the supplier's answer — the “How” (technical concept) and “With what” (resources/project plan); every PRD requirement must be covered. On customer acceptance, development starts.

Responsibility cascades: Customer (request) → Property Engineer (system properties) → Developer (component requirements). RM focuses on properties, not technical solutions.

Q2Explain the Kano model. Name its 5 levels with one example each, explain how a classification can change, and how the functional/dysfunctional survey works.
Type: theory / model · ~12 marks
✓ Model answer

The Kano model (Prof. Dr. Noriaki Kano, from quality management) prioritises customer requests by linking an implemented feature to customer satisfaction. Its 5 levels:

  • Basic needs (must): self-evident; only noticed when missing — e.g. a call-centre greeting.
  • Performance needs (should): satisfaction scales with fulfilment — e.g. hotline waiting time.
  • Delighter (could): unexpected, creates enthusiasm — e.g. live status tracking of a ticket.
  • Indifferent: little impact — e.g. the hold-music song.
  • Reverse (don't): its presence lowers satisfaction — e.g. jargon in a hotline call.

A classification changes by customer (delighter for one = reverse for another), by price/business model (delighter on a budget product = basic on a premium one), and over time (delighters become basic needs). The survey is standardized (to remove interviewer impact), includes demographics, and asks a functional question (“how would you feel if it had MORE of…?”) and a dysfunctional one (“…if it had LESS/none of…?”); the class is read from the answer combination (e.g. expect it / dislike it = basic; like it / dislike it = performance; like it / neutral = delighter).

Q3Mira orders a custom robot pet. (a) Why must a requirement be SMART, atomic and linked — give one failure example. (b) Why “specific” but not “too specific”? (c) What does RM do in tier-down and in validation/verification?
Type: application · ~8 marks
✓ Model answer

(a) A requirement must be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Reasonable, Time-Bound), atomic (one requirement per sentence), in active voice, free of weak words, and linked to its request, mother/child & neighbour requirements, functions, test items and interfaces. Failure example: a bridge built from Germany (North-Sea zero) and Switzerland (Mediterranean zero) had a 28 cm step because the height references were not linked — and the correction was even applied in the wrong direction.

(b) Natural language is ambiguous (“I saw the man on the mountain with the telescope” has many readings), so a requirement must be specific/testable (“efficient” → state 80%) — but not too specific: specify the problem, not the solution. Example: asked to reduce a bicycle's shipping damage, the team made a box that looks like a TV, cutting damage sharply — a solution that over-specification would have blocked.

(c) In tier-down, RM breaks system requirements to component level; the receiver must accept, reject or request a change, and only owns what it can build. In validation/verification, RM does not run tests but tracks whether each testable requirement was successfully confirmed.

Bonus: 10 rapid-fire short answers
  1. Requirement = exact development target for one property, all conditions.
  2. PRD = Lastenheft (customer, “what”); Impl. Spec = Pflichtenheft (supplier, “how/with what”).
  3. 6 phases = Collect · Assess · Develop · Tier-down · Verify · Changes.
  4. Kano 5 = basic · performance · delighter · indifferent · reverse.
  5. SMART = Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Reasonable · Time-Bound.
  6. NFR = non-functional req (e.g. 99.9% uptime), not a feature.
  7. LeLe rule: solution ≠ negation of the problem; anchor a concrete action.
  8. Active voice: “The motor rotates the shaft,” not “is driven by.”
  9. ReqIF = XML format (ProSTEP iViP) to exchange requirements.
  10. Avoid analysis paralysis — start realizing.
