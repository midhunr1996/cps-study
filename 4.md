## Fundamentals & Definition

### What "change management" is NOT (in this course)
- **Change management (CM)** in the *organizational* sense (German: *Veränderungsmanagement*) is the sum of all tasks concerning the change of **Processes, Behaviours, Strategy, Systems, Structures…** — focus on **organizational changes**.
- **For this course, that is *not* what we mean.**

### What we DO mean — Engineering Change Management
- **Engineering change management** (German: *Änderungsmanagement, ÄM*) is the process of **changing a product or parts of it** (from here on shortened to **change management — CM**).
- **Content:** changes of requests & requirements must be **aligned, tracked and followed up**.
- **Target:** **as few changes as possible** (keep product development stable) **but as many as needed** (to have a sellable product).

### Is a given change "change management"?
- A change that is **intended by the product** (e.g. a chameleon changing colour by design) → **not** change management.
- A change of the **product design itself** → **change management**.

## Effort, When, Where, For whom, Why

### Effort of CM
The **effort of a change increases** with:
- **System complexity** → try to reduce complexity (see *degree of connectivity* in "Basics…").
- **Point of detection** → detect as early as possible (early tests, virtual tests…) — see **Rule of Ten**.
- **Overlapping changes** → several changes running in parallel that affect or depend on each other.
- **Variants** → reduce as many variants as possible — see **variant management**.

### When to do CM? — 4 phases
Why not just stop after requirement management once the **PRD is frozen**? Because changes keep coming. CM runs across **four phases**:

| Phase | Description |
|---|---|
| **Until SOP** (start of production) | Main activities and **most changes**; the product-development period. |
| **Launch management** (shortly before SOP) | Critical time for changes, but still "have-to-be" needed. |
| **SOP → EOP** (end of production) | Main stage for **trouble-shooting** (quality issues) and **cost-down** measures. |
| **After EOP** | Recording of **field issues** and change of standards → better requirement set for the next product version. |

### Where to do CM?
Changes should be documented in the IT system at **two locations**:
1. On a **project-management level** — to aggregate the **whole** change (e.g. a new standard causing a change to both the metal sheet and the ECU software).
2. **Where the original object was created** — e.g. in a **PDM system** (product data management) for a single drawing change that causes a **BoM** (bill of materials) change.

> **!!! IMPORTANT !!!** Both locations must be **linked/connected**, otherwise this can cause a **branching of the data**.

### For whom to do CM?
Applicable to:
- **All internal products.**
- **"Buy" / supplied products** — the supplier must be **closely tracked and report all changes** (also part of **value chain management**).
- **Recommendation:** only nominate suppliers that are **ISO 9001 certified**, and **document the reporting duty in the supplier contract**.
- **Late changes cause high costs**, as contracts are fixed (…sometimes deliberately so by the supplier).

### Why CM?
- Enables the **"design for upgrade capability"** (see system wheel) → later HW/SW updates for the customer.
- Enables **traceability of changes** (versioning).
- Ensures a **releasable product** (product conformity).
- Keeps **costs in control**.
- Avoids an **increasing amount of work**.
- Avoids **breaking deadlines**.
- Avoids **issues during development**.
- Keeps **high product quality**.

## Level, Type & Size of changes

### Level of changes
- The **higher the level of change**, the **smaller the amount** of such changes (high-level changes are rare; low-level changes are frequent).

### Type of changes
- **Scope-Enlargement** — integration of **new** content (e.g. a new requirement).
- **Scope-Reduction** — **cancel** existing content (e.g. a no-longer-needed signature in a document).
- **Scope-Change** — **change** existing content (e.g. update of values, or a more precise description of a requirement).

### Size of change

| | **Evolutionary** | **Revolutionary / Disruptive** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Gradual change & improvement, optimizing the state of the art | Innovation or re-engineering; complete rethinking of the problem |
| Nature | Necessary in daily "doing" | A "radical" new solution |
| Risk | Risk of long-term failure if **not also** disruptive | Risk the product won't establish and keeps fundamentally changing |
| Example | A better phone | A smartphone instead of a telephone |

Graphically: evolution = **incremental steps** on technology A; disruption = a **big jump** to a new technology standard (technology B), over time.

## Version control & Document control

### Version control — motivation
- **Traceability** of the configuration over the **entire life cycle**:
  - frequency of changes visible,
  - evolution of the object (document, product…) visible,
  - **return to previous versions possible**.
- Connected to **configuration** and **variant** management.

### Version control — principles
- Product information changes constantly during development → the relevant objects change.
- **No "overwriting"** of objects — instead create a **new version** (visualises the object history; ensures the correct reference).
- **Marking of versioning:** version number is an **attribute** on the object (automatic counters in professional IT systems).
- An object is **clearly identified by the combination of ID/name + version number** — e.g. referencing requirement #073 alone is not enough (v1.0 or v1.1?).

### Version numbering schemes
- **Single-level numeric:** 0, 1, 2, 3…
- **Single-level alphabetical:** a, b, c, …, aa, ab, ac…
- **Two-stage:** two numbers/letters split by a "." — e.g. `a.0, a.1, a.2…b.0…` or `1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 2.0`. Used to make **smaller intermediate steps** visible.

**Typical semantic versioning** (`MAJOR.MINOR.MICRO`):
- **Major release** — significant changes (e.g. a whole re-design); number **before the "."**.
- **Minor release** — functional increase or improvements; number **directly after the "."**.
- **Micro release / patch level** — trouble-shooting.
- Example: from `3.9.4`, the next version is either `4.9.4` (major), `3.10.4` (minor — *not* `4.x`!), or `3.9.5` (micro).

### Version control — process
Similar to the "new requirements" process from the Requirement Management chapter:
`Start → new change into inbox/pool → change acceptable?` →
- **No** → change declined with reason → (modification possible? → yes: modify change & re-loop / no: discard change).
- **Yes** → **release new version → End**.

### Version control — content (what to document)
- Object that will be changed
- Status (approved, discarded…)
- Version number
- **What** was changed?
- **Why** was it changed?
- **For whom** (which product, from which date…) is the change relevant?
- **Who** made the change?
- **When** was it changed?
- **Who approved** the change?
- **When** was it approved?

### Document control — requirements (DIN EN ISO 9001)
Controlled and documented information per **ISO 9001** must be:
- **Available and suitable for use** where and when it is needed.
- **Adequately protected** (confidentiality, integrity…).
- Managed for **Distribution, Access, Discovery and Use**.
- **Filed/stored and preserved**, including **preservation of readability** (also covers operation).
- Subject to **monitoring of changes** (change history).
- **External documents must be marked.**

**Worked examples of the requirements:**
- *Available where needed:* keep occupational-safety instructions / manuals **directly at the machine** (so the operator always has access), **not** only on a digital drive.
- *Preservation of readability for ~30 years:* paper? Can you still read digital media (CD, USB stick, hard drive) — including the **file formats** — after 30 years?

### Example — drawing control
Transition from individual **2D drawings** (stored on a drive) to a **central drawing management** with: master data · drawing · permissions · change history · release status · creator · …

## Impact of changes

A change can cause:
- **Increased complexity** in the project because of variants and configurations.
  - *Example:* an endurance test started with the **old round plug**, but the whole tool kit now switches to **rectangular plugs**. Stop and redo the test? Change HW mid-test? Declare it "not relevant"? → can cause **quality issues** and **reduced customer satisfaction**.
- **Time delay.**
- **Change of cost / benefit ratio** (usually increased cost).
- **No longer following the contract (PRD).**
- **Worst case:** the change leads to the decision to **stop the whole project** (e.g. company strategy).

### Inversed Rule of Ten (for the *root cause*)
- The **"later" the root cause** of a failure sits in the system-design process (note: **the later the root cause, NOT the later the detection**), the **easier and cheaper** it is to change → the **"inversed rule of ten."**
- Ordered from cheap/late root cause to expensive/early root cause:
  - Update of the **design**
  - Update of the **assembly process** of the product
  - Change of the **concept**
  - The **test itself** needs to be redesigned (e.g. the test profile was too hard)
  - Change of the **whole system**

## Design, Concept & Requirement changes

### Design & concept change — caused by:
- **Failed tests** — the product does not fulfil one or more requirements → bad concept, design, or implementation needs changing.
- **Realization issues** — e.g. a planned **hardware** implementation does not work or is too costly → switch to a **software** implementation.
- A **re-design** — or, worse, a **new concept** — is needed to fulfil the requirements.

### Requirement changes
- In an ideal project there is a **hard PRD freeze**: **no requirement changes** allowed after that milestone.
- **Reality:** the PRD freeze is **partly soft** → changes are needed.

#### Reasons — two categories
- **"Have-to" changes** — no choice; **must be implemented** before product launch:
  - A **changing law** that becomes effective before launch.
  - A **failed verification** → change the **RM** (requirement) in case of "over-engineered expectations" (otherwise change the design/concept).
- **"Can-be" changes** — an **individual decision** whether to implement:
  - New / extended **applications** for the same product (e.g. use the wire not only in automotive but also in aeronautics).
  - **Company strategy.**
  - **Customer expectation** — in B2B, connected to **increased costs** (be aware — this is why RM matters for financial success).
  - **Tool kits** — e.g. switch all products from round plugs to rectangular plugs.
  - **Cultural or political expectations** — e.g. environment matters, so combustion engines are no longer welcome.

#### How to handle requirement changes
1. **Re-open** the requirement-management process and **re-assess** the request → **impact analysis** (resource demand and risks).
2. Create a **CR (Change Request)**.
3. The **CCB (Change Control Board)** — or, in a small company, the project leader — **decides & records** whether the late change is implemented (reasons for refusal → see "impact of changes").
4. **Prioritize** changes (cost/benefit) — which is implemented first.
5. **Plan** the implementation.

The **current valid configuration** = the **base line** (reference) **+ all CRs**. If the change is to be done:
- Update the requirements
- **Document** the changes (see versioning)
- **Implement** the change
- **Track the verification** of the change
- **Release** the change

## Product life cycle

- If the development process is passed **several times** for one product, each new cycle is a **new product generation**.
- The aim of **product lifecycle management (PLM/LCM)** is to **control** this process.

### Reasons for repeating the cycle
- **"Positive":** new customer requirements · intrinsic advancement · advancement of the state of the art.
- **"Negative":** competing products · quality issues · bad customer reviews · cost reduction.

### Networking of life cycles
- **Horizontal** — across **different generations** (Gen 1 → Gen 2). Usually only **forward-directed**; in rare cases a successor leads to changes in the predecessor (reasons: detected tool-kit defects, standardization of parts…).
- **Vertical** — across **different products** (Product A ↔ Product B). **Bidirectional.** Relevance depends on the **"technical proximity"** of products (e.g. car-experience transfers more to trucks than to bicycles, but "mobility behaviour" can be shared). Vertical connection also works **across generations**; the return path depends on each product's individual status.

### Branches
In practice the life cycle is **not linear**. Branches can:
- **continue linearly**
- **reunite (merge)**
- **run separately (split)**
- **die**

> IT support is recommended for complex product portfolios. Example of branching: the **Linux** distribution timeline.

### Cycle length
- Life cycles **vary in length**; the general trend is toward **shorter** life cycles (e.g. fast fashion), counteracted by **sustainability** initiatives.
- Duration depends on:
  - **Industry** — e.g. automotive ~7 years vs. smartphone ~1 year.
  - **New technologies** — accelerated by digital tools, slowed by increased complexity.
  - **Starting point** (see below).

### Starting point (derivation)
The starting point for a new product strongly affects the development-cycle duration:
- **New development** — no adoption of scopes from predecessors or other products.
- **Wide derivation** — basic features inherited from the predecessor.
- **Close derivation** — most of the development carried over with minor adjustments.

> **Example:** landline phone → (wide derivation, e.g. adopt SIM technology) → mobile phone → (close derivation, e.g. larger display) → smartphone.

### The product-life-cycle curve (phases)

| Phase | Name | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Investment | Product development, production setup, marketing starts toward the end. **High costs & uncertainty**; should be **as short as possible**. |
| **1** | Introduction | Sales rise slowly; **no profit yet** (development/marketing costs). Market decides acceptance; ends at **break-even** (reach it fast). |
| **2** | Growth | **Strong** sales increase; first revenues; continued high marketing. Should be **short & steep** (if production can follow). |
| **3** | Maturity | **Most profitable** phase; profits eventually fall (competition, price cuts); growth rate declines. Should be **as long as possible**. |
| **4** | Saturation | **No more growth**; sales start to fall; turnover & profits fall even further than sales. |
| **5** | Degradation | Profits **negative** (remaining costs); product should be **taken off the market**. |

Key markers on the curve: **market launch · break-even · EOS (end of sales)**, with **sales** and **revenue** plotted over time.

### Product life cycle management (LCM)
- Every product has its own life cycle (different length & character). The aim of **LCM** and product strategy is to **coordinate the different cycles** to avoid company fluctuations in:
  - **continuous utilization of production**,
  - **constant workload**,
  - **consistent revenue**, …
- **Bad timing:** introducing products **too quickly** one after another → high production load + a profit peak that then falls quickly; introducing a successor **too late** → a **"hole"**. → Aim for **coordinated life cycles**.

**LCM measures to lengthen / stimulate a cycle** (especially for cost-intensive products):
- **Product cost measures:**
  - Remove **functions not required** (focusing).
  - Remove **parts that won't be experienced**.
  - Reduce due to initially **too-high requirements** that proved irrelevant in the field (e.g. smaller semiconductors because customers use them less than intended).
- **Product enrichment** — new features.

**Why keep selling in degradation?** Retention of **long-term customers** · **cross-sales** (an "entry-ticket" product) · **shared investment costs** or **scale pricing** in purchasing across products.

## BCG Matrix

- **BCG = Boston Consulting Group.**
- Used to **display and analyze portfolios** and make **product decisions**.
- Serves **only for recording the actuals** — **not** for forecasting portfolios.
- Axes: **market growth** (vertical) × **relative market share** (horizontal).

### The four quadrants

| Quadrant | Market growth | Market share | Properties | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stars** ⭐ | Growing | High | Best product | Further **investment** from own cash flow, or skim **without** reducing market share |
| **Cash Cow** 🐄 | Low / stagnating | High | "Milking cows" | **"Milk" (skim)** — no discounts |
| **Question Marks** ❓ | Growth-potential | Low | New product | **Invest from other sources** or discontinue |
| **Poor Dog** 🐕 | Low / stagnating | Low | Discontinued product | **Terminate** — at the latest when contribution margin (Ger. *Deckungsbeitrag, DB*) is negative |

### Typical product life cycle through the matrix
**Question Marks → Stars → Cash Cow → Poor Dog.**

- **Question Marks** are necessary to create **future Stars** — otherwise nothing follows in the future.
- **Cash Cows** finance the investments.
- Products in the portfolio should **support each other**.

### Worked exercise (relative market share = company share ÷ competitor share)

| Product | Market growth | Sales volume | Share (company) | Share (competitor) | Relative share | Category |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 30% | 800.000 € | 15,0% | 10,0% | 1,50 | Star |
| B | 20% | 200.000 € | 25,0% | 20,0% | 1,25 | Star |
| C | 5% | 700.000 € | 17,0% | 10,0% | 1,70 | Cash Cow |
| D | 25% | 700.000 € | 7,5% | 15,0% | 0,50 | Question Marks |
| E | 5% | 300.000 € | 5,0% | 50,0% | 0,10 | Poor Dog |
| F | 10% | 300.000 € | 15,0% | 30,0% | 0,50 | Poor Dog |

Context: **Current GDP 3,88 bn · GDP forecast 4,46 bn → market-growth dividing line = 15,0 %.**

**Recommendation:** basically a good distribution → **"push" product D** with money from **product C** · **stop products E + F** · **launch a new product**.

## Configuration management

- **Configuration management — also "CM"** (German: *Konfigurationsmanagement*). **Do not mix up with change management!**
- Not limited to development — also runs **after SOP** (see "design for upgrade capability").
- Responsibility: a **configuration manager** (or the project lead in smaller organizations).
- **Difference vs change management** (interconnected, cannot be strictly separated): **change** focuses on the **single change/measure**; **configuration** focuses on the **interaction of changes** and the **resulting properties**.

### Targets of configuration management
- Handle the **increasing complexity of variants** (≠ variant management).
- **Manage configuration items** — software, hardware, or combinations.
- **Ensure performance, function, properties and integrity** of the system based on the combination of configuration items.
- **Document changes.**
- **Inform all relevant parties** about changes (transparency).
- Enable **concurrent & distributed engineering**.
- Enable **roll-backs** in case of issues.
- Create **reproducible** versions/configurations.

### The CM process — 5 steps
1. **CM organization** — organizational rules for CM: tools used · naming conventions · tasks · information duty · process definition & auditing · training.
2. **Configuration identification** (prerequisite for the rest) — select configuration units · organize them into a **product structure** · version control · document changes · define the **reference (base line)** · define the **granularity** of units (e.g. single screw vs. whole transmission).
3. **Configuration control** — **this is where change management takes place.**
4. **Configuration status accounting** — document the configuration & items, including resulting **system properties**, properties of single items, relevant items, and the **version** of single items.
5. **Configuration verification and audit** — **verify (test!)** that requirements are fulfilled, at whole-system level or per single configuration unit.

> **Worked example (base line + versions):** a system of *Inverter*, *E-Motor*, *Damping material*. Over a sequence of changes the items evolve, e.g. Inverter `1.0.0 → 1.1.0 → 1.2.0`, E-Motor `1.0.0 → 2.0.0 → 2.0.1`, Damping material `1.0.0 → 1.0.1`. Each combination is a defined configuration; document configuration numbers and their impact on cost & efficiency.

### Standards describing CM
- **ANSI & EIA** — *Configuration Management Standard EIA-649C*
- **Automotive SPICE** — Supporting Life Cycle Processes (**SUP**)
- **IEEE 828-2012** — Configuration Management in Systems and Software Engineering
- **ISO 10007** — Quality management — Guidelines for configuration management
- **NIST**
- CM is relevant for passing **ISO 26262 chapter 8** (road-vehicle functional safety) and **ISO 20000** (IT service management).

## Variant management

- **Variant management (VM):** the **business models** of the company define the system (product) **variants** to be developed.
- **Tasks of VM:**
  - **Transparency** about the variants.
  - **Optimizing** variants by creating new ones or **reducing (deleting)** them.
  - **Interconnection** with **LCM** and impact on **CM** (configuration management).
- **Target:** **as many variants as needed** for high revenue, **but as few as possible** because of complexity. Create **standard interfaces and tool kits** to keep complexity manageable in development, testing and production.

### How variants work
- The **same base product** "below the outer shape" → the same components are sourced more often → **cheaper per piece** (**economy of scale**).
- **Differentiation in the customer experience** by:
  - **Visual appearance** — HMI, colour, fonts, shape…
  - **Features and configurations** — e.g. personal vs. enterprise SW version.
- Enables economy of scale, but **causes more effort**.

### Where variants cause additional effort
- **Development** — what must change in the base variant to enable the variant and tool kits (synergy)? e.g. the variant needs more energy → the base variant needs a bigger battery; or use different configurations but with lower economy of scale.
- **Testing** — which tests can be **skipped** because the variant behaves like the base — **without** ignoring the different effects of the variants?
- **Sales** — adjust description & advertisement; how do variants divide between each other without "negativing" the others (e.g. a premium variant making others look bad)? Create **IT-configurators / decision helpers** so customers don't fail to decide because of too many variants.
- **General** — which variants are **not allowed** (e.g. a feature banned in a country)? Check whether new variants **cannibalize** existing sales (survey, market research…) before implementing.

### Increase vs decrease variants

| **Increase variants** | **Decrease variants** |
|---|---|
| Increasing customization | Reduce **costs** by creating **synergies** (e.g. one plug for all products) |
| Unlock new **markets** (US vs EU version) | (the opposite of the "increase" reasons) |
| Unlock new **clientele** (teenagers → silver agers) | |
| Unlock new **use cases** (craftsmanship → medical) | |

### Economy of scale & ROI
- A new variant should cause **low additional cost** — main production, logistics and development costs are already covered by the base variant.
- A variant has **fewer customers** (the main clientele is covered by the base variant), but the **ROI of a variant is typically higher** than the base.

### Business-case decision matrix (whether to implement a variant)

| # | Base variant | Derivation | Sum | Decision |
|:--:|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | positive | positive | positive | **Implement** the variant |
| 2 | positive | positive > base | positive | Implement; **check if the variant becomes the future base variant** |
| 3 | positive | negative | positive | Implement **only if** the variant's extra economy of scale is the reason the base's BC is positive |
| 4 | positive | negative | negative | **Don't implement**; check the base variant is still BC-positive |
| 5 | negative | positive | positive | **Implement** the variant |
| 6 | negative | positive | negative | **Stop the product**, optimize the base, or add more positive-BC variants |
| 7 | negative | negative | negative | **Stop the product** or optimize |

*(BC = business case; "derivation" = the new variant.)*

## Summary

- In an **ideal project, no change management is needed.**
- In reality, **proper change management is important** to:
  - Enable **traceability** of changes (versioning).
  - Ensure a **releasable product** (conformity).
  - Keep **costs in control**.
  - Avoid an increasing/unwanted **workforce**.
  - Avoid **breaking deadlines**.
  - Avoid **issues during development**.
  - Keep **high product quality**.
- ➡ **While change management is important, don't be afraid of changes.**
