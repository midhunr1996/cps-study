## Fundamentals

### Definition of risk
- **"Risk is the effect of uncertainty"** — definition in ISO 9001 [NM_ISO9001].
- Risk management's target is to **sort and structure (project) risks** in a structured way (see PDCA).
- Risks are **events or circumstances that can prevent achieving the project goal** (probability < 100%).
- If a risk has **already occurred**, it is considered a **problem** — risks and problems must be treated differently.
- Distinguish between **general company risks** and **project-specific risks**.

### PDCA (Deming cycle / Shewhart cycle)
According to ISO 9001 §0.3.2 [NM_ISO9001]. Also called the **Deming cycle** or **Shewhart cycle**.

- **Plan** — plan the process or product; set goals and resources; address risks and opportunities.
- **Do** — implementation of the plan.
- **Check** — monitoring and measuring the process or target condition.
- **Act** — take action to improve performance.

**Methods for the Plan phase:** FMEA (see *341_Example_FMEA_Bicycle*).

### General principles
- As part of risk management, **project opportunities** can also be recorded as **positive events**.
- It is advisable to set up a **"speak-up line"** so employees can **anonymously report** risks and violations that have not yet been discovered.
- Unlike costs, **risks do not add up** — they must be **aggregated using models and simulations** (e.g. **Monte Carlo simulation**).

### Risks of a company portfolio (diversification)
- There is an **efficiency curve**: optimum portfolios lie *on* the curve; portfolios cannot lie *above* it; portfolios *below* it are inefficient (same risk could yield higher return).
- Axes: **Revenue** vs **Risk (volatility)** — low / medium / high risk = low / medium / high volatility.
- Based on the **risk–return profile**, use **risk diversification** ("not all eggs in one basket").
- **Asset diversification** (different products) and **time diversification** reduce risk at the same return (Nobel Prize — **Harry Markowitz**).

**Worked principle — increasing reliability (crash-detection ECU at 50 %):**
- ❌ **Wrong:** add a *second identical ECU* — same solution to the same problem; this *increases* failure rate because now "guaranteed" one will fail.
- ✅ **Correct:** add a **mechanical crash detection** — a *diversified, non-correlating* solution.
- Rule: to reduce risk, implement **diversified and non-correlating** solutions.

### Risk management in standards
- **ISO 9001** requires risk management → **"risk-based thinking"**: what happens in the event of deviations? Use opportunities and prevent unwanted outcomes.
- **ISO 31000** — "Risk Management".
- **Provisions** (German: *Rückstellungen*) must be set aside for entrepreneurial and product risks → linked to **quality management**. *E.g.* if a 1 % risk of field failure is assumed, the corresponding repair costs for the portfolio must be provisioned.
- Risk management at company level is a **managerial task**.

### Risks affect different levels (examples)
- **Technical / product properties** — e.g. becomes too heavy
- **Compliance** — e.g. admission test failed; corruption
- **Finances / costs** — e.g. more expensive tools
- **Time** — e.g. delivery bottleneck
- **Market risk** — e.g. embargo
- **Currency risks** — e.g. appreciation/depreciation of currency pairs
- **Social risks** — e.g. demographic change
- **Project-management risks**
- **Environmental risks**

### Levels of risk management
- **6 levels** of risk management by **Gleißner & Mott** [FZ_Risikomanagment_Stufen].
- *KonTraG* — German law *"Gesetz zur Kontrolle und Transparenz im Unternehmensbereich"* — e.g. imposes a **duty on management to perform risk analysis**.

### Targets of risk management
1. **Detect** risks
2. **Analyse** risks
3. **Evaluate** risks
4. **Control** risks using:
   - **Avoid** (eliminate or "fallback")
   - **Prevent** (mitigate impact)
   - **Transfer**
   - **Accept**
5. **Monitor** risks (do they materialize?)
6. **Document and communicate** risks; define the organisation for risk management.

## Detecting Risks

### Detect risks
- Regular and systematic **internal and external analysis** of developments, events and changes.
- Frequency depends on the company's scope (low volatility vs fast-changing environment).

### Methods to detect (abstract)
- **Key performance indicator** changes
- **LeLe** (lessons learned — see earlier chapters)
- **Headstand / Flip-Flop** method
- **Delphi** method
- **Risk workshop**
- **PAAG / HAZOP** — *Prognose-Auffinden-Abschätzen-Gegenmaßnahme* / Hazard and Operability

### Head-stand / Flip-Flop method
1. **Invert the thesis** — e.g. instead of *"What can make our project fail?"* ask *"What must we do to make our project fail as quickly as possible?"*
2. **Find solutions** for the flipped thesis.
3. **Invert the solutions** of the inverted thesis.
4. **Define actions** out of the (re-inverted) solutions.

### Delphi method
1. Project leader explains the topic and targets to the experts.
2. Hand a worksheet to **each single expert** — **no discussion or exchange** between experts.
3. Experts **anonymously** hand in individual results to the project lead, who evaluates them.
4. **Major deviations** (incl. expert comments) are added to each handout and redistributed.
5. **Repeat steps 2–4** until consolidation is reached (within a defined window).
6. Build the **average** of the results.

### Other detection methods (safety management in development)
- **ALARP** — As Low As Reasonably Practicable
- **FMEA** — Failure Mode and Effects Analysis
- **DRBFM** — Design Review Based on Failure Mode

## Evaluating Risks

### Evaluation of risks
Two properties are assigned to **each** risk:
- **Impact / Severity** — how severe is it if the risk materialises?
- **Occurrence / Likelihood** (deliberately *not* "probability", which would imply a mathematical calculation rather than a **subjective assessment**) — how likely is it that the risk occurs?

Combining both gives a **risk matrix** used to **prioritise** risks. The resulting **risk list** is controlled and regularly updated by the project manager (in large projects, a dedicated **risk manager**).

### Evaluation of impact
To assess possible impacts/damage, use either:
- **Historical data** ("empirical values")
- A **probability distribution** (e.g. normal distribution)
- **Monte Carlo simulation**

### Severity scale (example)

| Rating | Meaning | Example |
|:---:|---|---|
| 1 | low | Customer-relevant function slightly below target. Cost/weight increase < 1 %. Postponement < 2 weeks. |
| 2 | medium | Customer-relevant function below target. Cost/weight increase < 5 %. Postponement < 1 month. |
| 3 | high | Important function significantly below setpoint. Cost/weight increase < 10 %. Postponement < 3 months. |
| 4 | very high | Important function cannot be represented at all. Cost/weight increase > 10 %. Postponement > 3 months. |

*Severity should be defined for each high-level property (see system wheel).*

### Occurrence scale (example)

| Occurrence | Rating | Meaning |
|---|:---:|---|
| 0 % | 0 | eliminated (no risk left) |
| > 0 % … ≤ 25 % | 1 | low |
| > 25 % … ≤ 50 % | 2 | medium |
| > 50 % … ≤ 75 % | 3 | high |
| > 75 % … < 100 % | 4 | very high |
| 100 % | 5 | occurred → hand over to problem management |

### Risk matrix

Risk level is read from **occurrence (columns)** × **severity (rows)**.

| Severity ↓ \ Occurrence → | 0 – eliminated (0 %) | 1 – low (≤25 %) | 2 – medium (≤50 %) | 3 – high (≤75 %) | 4 – very high (<100 %) | 5 – occurred (100 %) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 – low** | 0 eliminated | 1 low | 1 low | 1 low | 2 medium | 5 occurred |
| **2 – medium** | 0 eliminated | 1 low | 2 medium | 2 medium | 3 high | 5 occurred |
| **3 – high** | 0 eliminated | 1 low | 2 medium | 3 high | 4 very high | 5 occurred |
| **4 – very high** | 0 eliminated | 2 medium | 3 high | 4 very high | 4 very high | 5 occurred |

## Controlling Risks

### Risk avoidance
- Aim: use suitable measures to **reduce occurrence to 0**.
- Methods: prohibit certain activities/scopes, remove features from products, **technical barriers** (e.g. Poka Yoke).

### Poka Yoke (ポカヨケ)
- Japanese for **"avoid silly mistakes"**.
- Method for **immediate error detection and avoidance**.
- Uses technical measures to prevent misplacement, e.g. the **"key-lock" principle**.

### Risk prevention
- Aim: **reduce the occurrence** or **mitigate the severity** with suitable measures.
- Use process-optimisation and quality-management methods, e.g. **PDCA**.

### Risk transfer
- Shift risks to **other areas of the company, other companies, or other components** that can better deal with the risk.
- *E.g.* a crash ECU must survive a crash → would make the ECU massive → instead **shift the risk to the car chassis**.

### Risk acceptance
- Not all risks can be (economically) eliminated.
- Management or product development must then **consciously accept and support** them.
- Usually **only if occurrence and impact are low**.

## FMEA family

### FMEA — what it is
- **Failure Mode and Effects Analysis** (German: *Fehlermöglichkeiten- und Einflussanalyse*).
- A **reliability-improvement method**.
- Product defects are evaluated by **Severity** (customer perspective), **Probability**, and **Recognizability**.
- **Goals:**
  - Recognise systematic & random errors **before they occur**.
  - Derive measures for **detection & prevention**.
  - Improvement of successor products.

### FMEA — standards
- **Industry:** IEC 61508, ISO 13849
- **Automotive:** ISO 26262
- **Aerospace:** DO-254, ARP4761

### FMEA — variants & types
- **Variants:** FMEA · FMECA · FMEDA
- **Types of FMEA:** System FMEA · D-FMEA (Design) · P-FMEA (Process)

### FMEA — process
- Prepared in an **early phase** (even before the first detailed plans).
- Developed in **expert workshops from various domains** (see system wheel — electrical, mechanical, computer science…).
- Created **from the cause to the error**: what problems can arise from the error? E.g. *How does aging of the strut affect the crash-safety function? What happens to image recognition if a bit flips?*
- At the end: **verify the assumed probabilities** (were the assumptions correct?).

### FMEA — Rule of Ten
- The **later** an error is discovered, the **higher** the troubleshooting cost.
- For each stage the error stays undiscovered, costs increase by a factor of **~10**.
- → Focus must be on **planning and development**.
- Cost progression across stages: **Planning 1 € → Development 10 € → Assembly 100 € → Final acceptance 1 000 € → Customer** (each stage ×10).

### FMEA — changes
- The FMEA is a **"living" document**: maintain it during the project and afterwards up to **EOP** (End of Production), better until **EOS** (End of Sales).
- **Product changes must be updated in the FMEA.** A typical real-project error: the component is changed but the effects are no longer checked → the error then occurs **in the field**.
- The "old" FMEA is the basis for the **follow-up project** (knowledge transfer / LeLe).

### FMEA — System FMEA
- Considers the **overall system** and its underlying subsystems.
- Focus on **interfaces** and interaction of the overall system with the environment.
- Identifies **random and systemic** errors.

### FMEA — D-FMEA & P-FMEA
- **D-FMEA (Design FMEA):** used in development with **focus on the customer** — avoid systemic errors; determine suitability for manufacture and assembly; focus on individual parts/components that can cause failure.
- **P-FMEA (Process FMEA):** considers weaknesses in the **(production) process**; also uses findings from the D-FMEA.
- D-FMEA and P-FMEA should be **linked and interact**: a **critical characteristic in the design (product)** must be checked in the **process** → see **special characteristic (SC)** in design drawings.

### FMECA
- Extension of the FMEA to **"C – Critical"**.
- Same as FMEA, but **adds a metric for risk of failure** → enables **risk prioritisation**.
- When people say "FMEA", an **FMECA** is usually what is used.

### FMEDA
- Extension of the FMEA by **"D" for Diagnostics** — includes **multiple-error analysis**.
- Used in **automotive for electronic parts**.
- Accounts for the **reduction in failure rate** from diagnostic mechanisms (e.g. reading back output signals).
- Component data is expanded with **quantifiable error data** (failure probabilities, distribution of error states).
- Some systems can **detect and correct** errors; this ability is included when calculating the probability of failure.
- Improves analysis of **complex systems** and systems that use only a small part of their functionality in normal operation.

### RPV (Risk Priority Value)
RPV combines the three FMEA ratings (Probability × Severity × Recognizability), each scored **1–10**.

#### Probability scale (1–10)

| Probability of failure | Failure rate | Rating |
|---|---|:---:|
| always (error inevitable) | ≥ 1 in 2 (50 %) | 10 |
| almost always | ≥ 1 in 3 (33.3 %) | 9 |
| very often (occurs repeatedly) | ≥ 1 in 8 (12.5 %) | 8 |
| often | ≥ 1 in 20 (5 %) | 7 |
| regularly (occasional errors) | ≥ 1 in 80 (1.25 %) | 6 |
| repeated | ≥ 1 in 400 (0.25 %) | 5 |
| sometimes | ≥ 1 in 2 000 (0.05 %) | 4 |
| rarely (rare errors) | ≥ 1 in 15 000 (0.0067 %) | 3 |
| very rare | ≥ 1 in 150 000 (0.00067 %) | 2 |
| almost never | < 1 in 150 000 (0.00067 %) | 1 |

#### Severity scale (1–10)

| Relevance of the failure | Description | Rating |
|---|---|:---:|
| dangerous / illegal | Danger or violation of laws, serious injuries, death. Significant security flaws. | 10 |
| critical | Danger or violation of laws possible. Very important. Serious functional impairment and safety deficiencies. | 9 |
| fatal | Total functional failure / loss of primary functions. Customer very upset. Fatal, critical error. | 8 |
| serious | Functions severely restricted. Customer upset. Critical error. | 7 |
| significant | Failure of individual main functions. Customer quite upset. Loss of a comfort function/feature. | 6 |
| clearly | Moderate limitation of usability. Customer a little upset. Reduction of a comfort function/feature. | 5 |
| recognizable | Use little limited. Customer disgruntled. Minor functional impairment. | 4 |
| slightly | Use slightly limited. Customer slightly upset. Minor appearance/sound impairment. | 3 |
| imperceptibly | Very low impact. Customer hardly touched. Barely noticeable visual/sound impairment. | 2 |
| no | Customer does not notice the impact. No impairment. | 1 |

#### Recognizability scale (1–10)

| Recognizability | Probability to detect the failure by the designed concept | Rating |
|---|---|:---:|
| certainly not recognized | < 10 % — concept CANNOT recognise potential causes/mechanisms and resulting errors. | 10 |
| very unlikely | ≥ 10 % | 9 |
| unlikely | ≥ 33 % | 8 |
| very low | ≥ 65 % | 7 |
| low | ≥ 73 % | 6 |
| medium | ≥ 80 % | 5 |
| medium high | ≥ 84 % | 4 |
| high | ≥ 90 % | 3 |
| very high | ≥ 95 % | 2 |
| surely recognized | ≥ 99 % — concept reliably recognises potential causes/mechanisms and resulting errors. | 1 |

### FMEA — Special Characteristic (SC)
- **High severity** leads to a **Special Characteristic (SC)**, which must be **noted on drawings** and given special attention in production (e.g. **each SC must be checked/measured**).
- Be careful **not** to declare "everything" an SC — that causes high effort and cost in production.
- An SC can also be a **regulatory requirement** (e.g. product labelling, without which selling would be illegal).
- **Automotive implementation variants:**
  - **BMW:** L, S, F features — **L**egal (approval/homologation), **S**afety, **F**unction (function/production).
  - **Daimler:** DS and DZ characteristics (documentation for safety relevance / for certification relevance).
  - **VW:** D and S features (must be **D**ocumented and relevant to **S**afety).

## Exercises (self-test)

**1. Head-stand method (groups, 15 min).**
Use the head-stand method to find possible risks for: *"How can we ensure that our autonomous driving robot does not collide with an obstacle?"*
Steps: invert the thesis → find solutions for the flipped thesis → invert those solutions → define actions.

**2. Delphi method (groups).**
*"We will open an agency for process and metrology consulting. How much € should we charge per hour?"* You are the experts — you may use the Internet, **but do not exchange with others**. Then show the **lowest and highest** votes (not all) and gather feedback from those experts.

**3. Risk classification (groups, 10 min).** Classify each event by **P** (occurrence) and **S** (severity):

| Event | P | S | Result |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Hit by lightning in an open field | 1 | 4 | medium |
| Electrostatic discharge when walking with shoes on carpet | 4 | 1 | medium |
| Cut your finger when finely slicing a potato | 1 | 3 | low |
| Win the jackpot in the German "6 aus 49" lottery | — | — | **not a risk — a chance!** |
| Walking on the left lane of the Autobahn in daytime | 4 | 4 | very high |
| Arcing when opening an electrical circuit | ? | 4 | clarification needed (too little info on P) |

**4. FMECA (3 groups, ~1 h).** Create an FMECA for — Group 1: digital voltmeter · Group 2: hand mixer · Group 3: swing. Present results to another group for feedback (2×10 min), then summarise (10 min): *What are the advantages of an FMECA and what are the problems with creating one?*

## Summary

- Risk management is necessary to **avoid greater damage** to companies and products.
- Risks **can and should be identified as early as possible** (Rule of Ten).
- Based on **severity and occurrence**, risks are rated within a **risk list**.
- Especially in development and production, each product must be analysed using **FMEA**, and **special characteristics (SC)** must be derived from it.
- > *"Nothing happens without risk, but without taking risks also nothing happens."* — Walter Scheel, former President of Germany. While risk management is important, don't forget to **take risks to step forward**.
