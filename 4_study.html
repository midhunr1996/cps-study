📖 The Full Story — “Ada and the Ever-Changing Car”

The whole chapter as one connected tale. Don't try to memorize it — just read it like a story and let it run as a film in your head. Because each thing happens for a reason, the concepts stick by themselves. Exam words are in bold; the table at the end maps every beat to the point you write in the exam.

Meet Ada, keeper of an electric car called “Volt.” Its blueprint was frozen long ago — the PRD freeze — but the real world keeps poking it: a new law, a failed test, a supplier who swaps a plug. Ada's job is the opposite of Riku's detective work: there they stopped things going wrong; here Ada manages the things that must change anyway. Her motto is carved above her desk — “as few changes as possible, but as many as needed.”

First she's clear about what her job is not. A chameleon suns itself on Volt's hood, flushing from green to red — but it was built to do that, so that's no change management. The moment someone re-draws the car's actual blueprint, that is engineering change management — the process of changing a product or its parts (not the organizational kind — changing people's behaviour and strategy — which this course ignores).

She keeps four cousins straight by tipping out Russian dolls, biggest to smallest: the whole fleet of variants (Variant Management) holds each model-year generation E90→F30→G20 (Lifecycle Management), which holds a HW+SW combo like “software 3.7.2 on the d-sample board” (Configuration Management), which holds one tiny circuit board (Change Management). The smaller the doll, the more of them there are — and beware the two “CM”s, Configuration and Change, doing different jobs.

Ada interrogates every change like a journalist — how hard, when, where, for whom, why. The effort grows under four weight-plates: system complexity, late detection, overlapping parallel changes, and variants (“tangled, late, crowded, many”). The when chimes four times across Volt's life: most changes land before SOP (start of production), a few critical ones at launch, then SOP→EOP brings trouble-shooting and cost-down, and after EOP you record field issues to improve the next product. The where is two filing drawers chained together — the project-management level (the whole change) and the PDM system where the original object lives — and the chain is the point: unlink them and the data branches. The for whom covers internal products and suppliers, who must be ISO 9001-certified and contractually bound to report every change. And the why: upgrade capability, traceability, a releasable conformant product, cost control and high quality.

Every change also gets sorted three ways: by level (the higher the level, the fewer there are), by type — a balloon you inflate (scope-enlargement), pop a piece off (scope-reduction) or squeeze into a new shape (scope-change) — and by size: a tortoise's evolutionary baby-steps (a better phone) versus a rocket's disruptive leap (a smartphone replacing the telephone).

Ada's golden discipline is her logbook: never erase — always add a new version. Each object is named by ID + version number, so “requirement #073” is meaningless without “v1.0 or v1.1.” Pro tools count versions semantically as MAJOR.MINOR.MICRO — a re-design before the first dot, a feature in the middle (which can roll past 9, so 3.9.4 → 3.10.4, not 4.x!), and a bug-fix at the end. Each version logs a detective's W-form: what changed, why, who, when, for whom, and who approved it when. On top sits document control (ISO 9001): information must be available where it's needed (the safety manual at the machine, not buried on a drive), protected, and readable in 30 years (will that old CD format still open?).

Why so careful? Because one little change ripples — tangling variants, delaying time, raising cost, breaking the PRD contract, and at worst killing the project. Here lurks the chapter's twist, the inversed Rule of Ten: the normal rule said the later you detect a fault, the dearer; this one says the later the root cause sits in the design, the cheaper to fix — a snowball rolling up the hill, shrinking. Updating a design is cheap; re-doing the whole concept or the entire test is what costs you.

The frozen blueprint cracks for two kinds of reason. “Have-to” changes give you no choice — a new law before launch, or a failed verification forcing you to relax an over-engineered requirement. “Can-be” changes are your call — new applications, company strategy, a customer's shifting expectation, a tool-kit swap (round plugs → rectangular), cultural or political shifts. Either way Ada runs the same path: re-open the requirement → impact analysis → raise a Change Request (CR) → the Change Control Board (CCB) decides and records → prioritize by cost/benefit → plan the implementation (a valid configuration = the base line + all approved CRs).

Volt itself rides a roller-coaster, the product life-cycle curve in six phases: (0) Investment (pay to climb, keep it short), (1) Introduction (slow climb to break-even), (2) Growth (steep up), (3) Maturity (the top — most profit — stretch it as long as possible), (4) Saturation (rolling down), (5) Degradation (off the rails — take it off the market). Running the whole ride again is a new generation, and Lifecycle Management coordinates every product's cycle so the company has no holes and no overload. To survey her whole farmyard of products, Ada uses the BCG matrix — plotting market growth against relative market share — sorting each into a shooting ⭐ Star (invest), a 🐄 Cash Cow (milk it, no discounts), a ❓ Question Mark (a new arrival — fund it or drop it) or a 🐕 Poor Dog (terminate when its margin turns negative). It only records the actual picture; it never forecasts. The usual life-story runs ❓ → ⭐ → 🐄 → 🐕: question-marks grow into stars, and cash cows pay the bills (relative market share = your share ÷ the rival's).

Two last tools snap together. Configuration Management locks chosen LEGO bricks — one inverter version, one motor, one damping — into a single working combo and records exactly which bricks (config cares about the combo's properties; change cares about the single brick). It even runs after SOP for upgrades, in five steps O-I-C-S-V: Organization → Identification → Control (where the change actually happens!) → Status-accounting → Verification/audit (its standards — EIA-649C, Automotive SPICE, IEEE 828 — are needed to pass ISO 26262 ch.8 and ISO 20000). And Variant Management lines up the same Volt in several paint-and-trim versions over one shared base — buying parts in bulk for economy of scale — aiming for as many variants as revenue needs, as few as complexity allows, via standard interfaces & tool kits. Each candidate variant faces a see-saw: weigh the base business-case against the derivation's, then read the SUM — positive means build it, negative means stop or optimize (the trap is case 3: a money-losing variant stays only if its volume is what keeps the base profitable).

The moral (your essay close): in an ideal project no change management is needed — but in reality it guarantees traceability, a releasable product, cost control and high quality. The spirit is always “as few changes as possible, but as many as needed” — and, as the closing slide insists, don't be afraid of changes.

01What is change management? (and what it is NOT)

🧒 Story
A chameleon sits on Volt's hood, changing colour — but it was built to do that, so that's not change management. Re-drawing the car's actual blueprint is. Careful: the word also means the organizational/people kind (behaviours, strategy, structures) — but in this course we ignore that. We mean engineering change management = changing a product or its parts.
📝 Write in exam — Definition
  • Engineering change management (Ger. Änderungsmanagement, ÄM) = the process of changing a product or parts of it.
  • The other CM (Ger. Veränderungsmanagement) = organizational change (processes, behaviours, strategy, systems, structures) → not our topic.
  • Content: changes of requests & requirements must be aligned, tracked, followed up.
  • Target: as few changes as possible (stable development) but as many as needed (sellable product).
🧠
Chameleon changes on purpose = no CM. Re-paint the blueprint = CM. Goal = “few but enough.”

02Four cousins, all about “what object?”

🧒 Story
Tip out Russian dolls, biggest to smallest: the whole fleet of variants (VM), a model-year generation E90→F30→G20 (LCM), a HW+SW combo (Config-CM), and the tiniest doll, one circuit board (Change-CM). The smaller the doll, the MORE of them there are.
📝 Write in exam — object increases top→down
  • Variant management (VM): products/variants — e.g. sedan, convertible.
  • Product life cycle management (LCM): new generations — e.g. E90→F30→G20.
  • Configuration management (CM): configurations = HW+SW combos — e.g. ECU SW 3.7.2 + d-sample PCB.
  • Change management (CM): components — e.g. d-sample PCB.
🧠
Dolls shrink V → L → C → C; counts grow. Two “CM”s — Configuration vs Change — don't mix!

03The 5 W's of change (Effort · When · Where · For whom · Why)

🧒 Story
Ada interrogates every change like a journalist: how hard, when, where, for whom, why.
📝 Write in exam
  • Effort grows with: system complexity · late point of detection (Rule of Ten) · overlapping (parallel) changes · variants.
  • When — 4 phases: Until SOP (most changes) → Launch management (just before SOP, critical) → SOP→EOP (trouble-shooting + cost-down) → After EOP (record field issues, improve next product).
  • Where — 2 linked locations: (1) project-management level (whole change) + (2) where the original object was made (e.g. PDM → drawing/BoM). Both MUST be linked, else the data branches.
  • For whom: all internal AND bought/supplied products; track suppliers, nominate only ISO 9001-certified ones, put the reporting duty in the contract.
  • Why: design-for-upgrade capability · traceability (versioning) · releasable/conform product · cost control · avoid extra work/missed deadlines/dev issues · high quality.
🧠
Effort = “Tangled, Late, Crowded, Many.” When = Build·Launch·Fix·Learn. Where = two drawers, one chain.

04Level, Type & Size of a change

🧒 Story
Sort every change three ways — how high-level, what kind, how big a leap. A balloon you can inflate, pop, or reshape; a tortoise step or a rocket jump.
📝 Write in exam
  • Level: the higher the level of change, the fewer there are.
  • 3 Types: Scope-Enlargement (add new content) · Scope-Reduction (cancel content) · Scope-Change (change existing content).
  • 2 Sizes: Evolutionary = gradual improvement (better phone; risk = long-term failure if never disruptive). Revolutionary/Disruptive = radical rethink (smartphone vs telephone; risk = never establishes).
🧠
Type = Inflate · Pop · Reshape. Size = Tortoise tiptoe vs rocket leap.

05Version control & Document control (the logbook)

🧒 Story
Ada's golden rule: never erase — always add a new version. Her logbook shows the object's whole history so anyone can roll back. Each object is named by ID + version (requirement #073 isn't enough — v1.0 or v1.1?).
📝 Write in exam — Version control
  • Motivation: traceability over the whole life cycle (change frequency & object evolution visible; roll-back possible).
  • No overwriting → create a new version; version number is an attribute (auto-counter in pro tools).
  • Identify = ID/name + version number together.
  • Schemes: single-level numeric (0,1,2…) · alphabetical (a,b,…,aa,ab…) · two-stage (a.0…b.0 or 1.1,1.2,2.0).
  • Semantic MAJOR.MINOR.MICRO: Major = re-design (before the dot) · Minor = feature (after the dot) · Micro = patch. From 3.9.4 → 4.9.4 (major) / 3.10.4 (minor — not 4.x!) / 3.9.5 (micro).
  • Process: like “new requirements” — change into pool → acceptable? → no: decline w/ reason (or modify & re-loop) → yes: release new version.
  • Content to log: object · status · version no. · What · Why · For-whom · Who · When · Who-approved · When-approved.
📝 Write in exam — Document control (ISO 9001)
  • Information must be available where/when needed · protected (confidentiality, integrity) · managed for distribution/access/discovery/use · stored with preserved readability · change-monitored · external docs marked.
  • Available where needed → safety manuals at the machine, not just on a drive.
  • Readability for 30 years → can you still read that CD/USB and its file format later?
🧠
Version dials = big·middle(rolls past 9!)·small. Content = detective's W-form. Docs = right place · protected · readable in 30 yrs · external = stamped.

06Impact of changes (+ the INVERSED Rule of Ten)

🧒 Story
One little change ripples everywhere. Example: you started the endurance test with the old round plug, now everything switches to rectangular plugs — stop & redo? swap mid-test? ignore it?
📝 Write in exam — Impact
  • Increased complexity (variants/configs) → quality issues & unhappy customers · time delay · worse cost/benefit · breaks the PRD · worst case the whole project is stopped.
📝 Write in exam — Inversed Rule of Ten
  • The later the root cause (NOT the later the detection!) sits in the design process, the cheaper & easier to change.
  • Cheap (late root cause) → expensive (early root cause): update design → update assembly process → change concept → redesign the test → change the whole system.
🧠
A snowball rolling UP the hill shrinks — late root cause = small fix. (The inverse of the normal ❄️ snowball.)

07Design, Concept & Requirement changes

🧒 Story
The frozen blueprint cracks. Two crack-types: ones you have no choice about, and ones you choose.
📝 Write in exam
  • Design & concept change — caused by: failed tests · realization issues (HW won't work / too costly → switch to SW) · needing a re-design or new concept.
  • PRD freeze: ideal = hard (no changes after); reality = soft → changes happen.
  • “Have-to” changes (no choice, before launch): a new law effective before launch · a failed verification → relax an over-engineered requirement (else change design/concept).
  • “Can-be” changes (your call): new/extended applications · company strategy · customer expectation (B2B = extra cost) · tool kits (round→rectangular plugs) · cultural/political (no combustion engines).
  • How to handle: re-open requirement process → impact analysis (resources + risks) → create a CR → CCB/project lead decides & records → prioritize (cost/benefit) → plan. Valid config = base line + all CRs; then update → document → implement → track verification → release.
🧠
Cracks = “have-to” (law / failed test) vs “can-be” (your call). Path: CR → CCB → prioritize → plan.

08Product life cycle (the roller-coaster)

🧒 Story
Volt rides a roller-coaster: you pay to climb, you peak and earn, then you roll down and get scrapped. Run the whole ride again = a new generation (managed by PLM/LCM).
📝 Write in exam
  • Repeat reasons: Positive — new customer requirements, intrinsic advancement, state-of-the-art advances. Negative — competing products, quality issues, bad reviews, cost reduction.
  • Networking: Horizontal = across generations (usually forward-only). Vertical = across products, bidirectional, strength = “technical proximity” (cars→trucks > cars→bicycles).
  • Branches (not linear): continue · merge · split · die (e.g. Linux distribution timeline).
  • Cycle length: trend = shorter (fast fashion) vs sustainability. Depends on industry (auto ~7 yrs vs phone ~1 yr), new technology, and starting point.
  • Starting point / derivation: New development (nothing reused) · Wide derivation (basic features inherited) · Close derivation (most carried over). E.g. landline → (wide: SIM) → mobile → (close: bigger display) → smartphone.
🧠
Roller-coaster phases below. Horizontal = generations (forward); Vertical = products (both ways).

09BCG Matrix (the farmyard)

🧒 Story
A farmyard of products: a shooting Star, a Cash Cow to milk, a Question Mark new arrival, a sad Poor Dog. Boston Consulting Group plots them on market growth × relative market share. It only records the actual situation — it does not forecast.
📝 Write in exam
  • ⭐ Stars — high growth, high share, best product → invest (own cash flow) or skim without losing share.
  • 🐄 Cash Cow — low growth, high share → “milk” (skim), no discounts.
  • ❓ Question Marks — high-growth, low share, new product → invest from other sources or discontinue.
  • 🐕 Poor Dog — low growth, low share, discontinued → terminate (latest when contribution margin / DB < 0).
  • Typical order: ❓ → ⭐ → 🐄 → 🐕. Question-marks make future stars; cash cows finance; products support each other.
  • Relative market share = company share ÷ competitor share (>1 = you lead).
🧠
Farmyard: Star → Cow → Question → Dog. Growth ↑ × your-share-vs-rival.

10Configuration management (snap the LEGO together)

🧒 Story
Ada snaps the chosen LEGO bricks (one inverter version + one motor version + one damping version) into a single working combo and writes down exactly which bricks. Configuration management cares about the combo's properties; change management cares about the single brick. (Same “CM” — different job!) It runs even after SOP, owned by a configuration manager.
📝 Write in exam
  • Targets: handle variant complexity · manage configuration items (HW/SW/combos) · ensure system performance/function/integrity · document changes · inform everyone (transparency) · enable concurrent/distributed engineering · enable roll-backs · create reproducible configurations.
  • 5-step process (O-I-C-S-V): ① Organization (rules, tools, naming, training) ② Identification (units, product structure, base line, granularity) ③ Control ← change management happens HERE ④ Status accounting (document config + item properties/versions) ⑤ Verification & audit (test the requirements).
  • Standards: EIA-649C · Automotive SPICE (SUP) · IEEE 828-2012 · ISO 10007 · NIST. Needed to pass ISO 26262 ch.8 (vehicle FuSi) & ISO 20000 (IT service mgmt).
🧠
LEGO combo = config; one brick = change. Process = O-I-C-S-V (control = where change lives).

11Variant management (paint-swatch versions)

🧒 Story
Ada lines up the same Volt in several paint-and-trim versions. Underneath, the same base product → parts bought in bulk → economy of scale. Customers see different looks (HMI, colour, shape) and features (personal vs enterprise). More variants = more money but more effort.
📝 Write in exam
  • Basics: business model defines the variants. Tasks: transparency · optimize (create/delete) · link with LCM, impact config-CM. Target: as many as needed for revenue, as few as possible for complexity → standard interfaces & tool kits.
  • Extra effort by area: Development (what must the base change?) · Testing (which tests can be skipped safely?) · Sales (don't let variants “negative” each other; build IT-configurators) · General (banned variants? cannibalization?).
  • Increase variants → customization · new markets (US vs EU) · new clientele (teens→silver agers) · new use cases (craft→medical). Decrease → cut cost via synergies (one plug for all).
  • Economy & ROI: new variant = low extra cost (base covers it), fewer customers, but ROI usually higher than the base.
🧠
Same base under the shell = economy of scale. Target = as needed / as few as possible with tool kits.

4+−−Don't implement; check the base is still BC-positive
5−++Implement the variant
6−+−Stop the product / optimize base / add positive-BC variants
7−−−Stop the product or optimize

Read the SUM column: positive = go, negative = stop. Case 3 is the trap — a “−” variant stays if it props up the base's economy of scale.

3 Practice Questions + model answers

Try to answer on paper before clicking Reveal. The model answers show the exact points an examiner rewards.

Q1Why is change management needed even after the PRD freeze? Describe when (the phases) and where it is done, and how a requirement change is processed.
Type: structured / theory · ~10 marks
✓ Model answer

The ideal hard PRD freeze is in reality soft: laws change, tests fail, suppliers swap parts. The goal is “as few changes as possible, but as many as needed” (stable development, yet a sellable product).

  • When — 4 phases: until SOP (most changes), launch management (just before SOP, critical), SOP→EOP (trouble-shooting + cost-down), after EOP (record field issues to improve the next product).
  • Where: documented at two linked IT locations — the project-management level (whole change) and where the original object was made (e.g. a PDM system). Both must be linked or the data branches.
  • Requirement change process: re-open the requirement process → impact analysis (resources + risks) → create a CR → the CCB (or project lead) decides & records → prioritize (cost/benefit) → plan. The valid configuration = base line + all CRs; then update, document (versioning), implement, track verification, release.

Changes are either “have-to” (new law, failed verification) or “can-be” (new applications, strategy, customer expectation, tool kits, cultural/political).

Q2Distinguish change management, configuration management, variant management and life-cycle management. Then list the 5 steps of the configuration-management process and say where change actually happens.
Type: theory / definitions · ~12 marks
✓ Model answer

They differ by the object they manage (object count grows down the list):

  • Variant management (VM): products/variants (sedan, convertible).
  • Life-cycle management (LCM): new generations (E90→F30→G20); coordinates all cycles to avoid “holes”.
  • Configuration management (CM): HW+SW configurations and their resulting properties.
  • Change management (CM): a single component / single change & measure.

Configuration vs change: change focuses on the single change; configuration on the interaction of changes and resulting properties.

CM 5-step process (O-I-C-S-V): ① CM organization, ② configuration identification (units, product structure, base line, granularity), ③ configuration control — change management happens here, ④ status accounting, ⑤ verification & audit (test the requirements). Standards: EIA-649C, Automotive SPICE (SUP), IEEE 828-2012, ISO 10007 — relevant for ISO 26262 ch.8 and ISO 20000.

Q3(a) Sketch the product life-cycle curve and name its phases. (b) Explain the BCG matrix and its typical order. (c) A new variant has a positive base BC, a negative derivation BC, but a positive sum — what do you do, and why?
Type: application · ~8 marks
✓ Model answer

(a) Phases over time (sales/revenue): 0 Investment (high cost, short) → 1 Introduction (slow, to break-even) → 2 Growth (steep) → 3 Maturity (most profitable, long) → 4 Saturation (sales fall) → 5 Degradation (negative profit, take it off the market). Key markers: market launch, break-even, EOS.

(b) The BCG matrix (Boston Consulting Group) plots products on market growth × relative market share to record the actual portfolio (not a forecast): Stars (invest/skim), Cash Cows (milk), Question Marks (invest from elsewhere or drop), Poor Dogs (terminate). Typical order: Question Mark → Star → Cash Cow → Poor Dog; question-marks create future stars, cash cows finance them.

(c) This is case 3: implement the variant only if the variant's additional economy of scale is the reason the base variant's business case is positive — i.e. removing the derivation would raise the base's costs and worsen the overall sum. Otherwise don't.

Bonus: 10 rapid-fire short answers
  1. Engineering CM = the process of changing a product/parts (Ger. ÄM).
  2. Two “CM”s: Configuration vs Change — don't mix.
  3. Effort grows with: complexity, late detection, overlapping changes, variants.
  4. Where to log: project level + PDM — linked, or data branches.
  5. 3 types of change: enlargement, reduction, change.
  6. Versioning: MAJOR.MINOR.MICRO — 3.9.4 → 3.10.4 (minor, not 4.x).
  7. Inversed Rule of Ten: later root cause = cheaper fix.
  8. BCG limit: records actuals only, not a forecast.
  9. Config control is where change management takes place.
  10. Variant target: as many as needed, as few as possible (tool kits).
